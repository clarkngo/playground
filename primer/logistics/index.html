Industry Overview
Logistics is the physical backbone of commerce — coordinating transport, warehousing, customs, and last-mile delivery across a $10T global market. This primer focuses on integrated logistics (3PL, warehousing, last-mile, supply chain orchestration), distinct from pure maritime container shipping. Maersk's transformation from ocean carrier to integrated logistics provider is a central case study.
🏭

Third-Party Logistics / 3PL

Outsourced logistics handling transport, warehousing, and fulfillment. DHL Supply Chain, XPO, Ryder, DB Schenker are leaders. Asset-light vs asset-heavy models define margin profiles. 3PL market approximately $1.3T globally.

🚚

Freight Brokerage & Road Transport

Truck brokerage connecting shippers with carriers. C.H. Robinson, Echo Global, Uber Freight, Convoy (acquired). Truckload (TL) vs less-than-truckload (LTL). Spot vs contract rates drive margin volatility for brokers.

📦

Integrated Express & Parcels

FedEx, UPS, DHL Express — door-to-door express for parcels and documents. Time-definite delivery. Revenue from weight/zone/speed matrix. Serves both B2B and B2C customers globally.

🏠

Last-Mile Delivery

Final delivery to consumer door — the most expensive leg, approximately 50% of total logistics cost. Amazon Logistics (AMZL), DoorDash Drive, Uber Direct. Gig-economy delivery fleets. Route optimization is critical to unit economics.

🏗️

Warehousing & Fulfillment

Maersk warehousing, Prologis (REIT), Amazon FBA. Pick/pack/ship operations. WMS systems manage inventory. Cold chain for pharma and food. Returns and reverse logistics. 3D inventory tracking.

🌐

Integrated Logistics

Maersk transformation: ocean + inland + warehousing + customs + last-mile under one P&L. Competing with DHL, Kuehne+Nagel for integrator share. "Integrator" vs "freight forwarder" — integrators own assets across the chain.

Key Terminology
Essential vocabulary for logistics — operations, technology, finance, and trade concepts.
3PL (Third-Party Logistics)Operations▼
Outsourced logistics provider handling transport, warehousing, and/or fulfillment. 4PL adds a supply chain management and orchestration layer — the 4PL manages multiple 3PLs on behalf of the shipper. Asset-heavy 3PLs own trucks and warehouses; asset-light 3PLs broker capacity.
Freight ForwarderTrade▼
Arranges international shipments on behalf of shippers. Manages bill of lading, customs documentation, and multimodal coordination. Does not own transport assets (acts as NVOCC for ocean or air freight agent). Flexport is a technology-first freight forwarder.
TL / LTL (Truckload / Less-than-Truckload)Operations▼
TL = full truck dedicated to one shipper for a single move. LTL = multiple shippers share truck capacity, paying for their portion. LTL carries 150 to 10,000 lb shipments and uses terminal networks to consolidate and deconsolidate freight. LTL pricing uses freight classification (NMFC).
DrayageOperations▼
Short-distance trucking, typically moving containers from port to warehouse or rail yard to warehouse. Critical for port congestion relief. Drayage capacity is a frequent bottleneck during peak shipping seasons. Often performed by owner-operators.
POD (Proof of Delivery)Operations▼
Delivery confirmation — typically a signature or photo captured via driver app. Key billing trigger for many logistics contracts: payment is released upon POD. Digital POD eliminates paper-based disputes and accelerates cash collection.
Lead TimeSupply Chain▼
Total time from order placement to delivery at the destination. Supply chain optimization targets lead time reduction. Long lead times require higher safety stock investment. Amazon Prime reset consumer expectations for lead time to 1-2 days.
Safety StockSupply Chain▼
Buffer inventory held to absorb demand variability or supply disruptions. Calculated using demand standard deviation and lead time variability. Higher safety stock reduces stockouts but ties up working capital. COVID-19 exposed widespread under-investment in safety stock.
SKU (Stock Keeping Unit)Warehouse▼
Unique identifier for each distinct product variant (size, color, configuration). Warehouse management tracks inventory at SKU level. SKU proliferation increases picking complexity and slotting challenges. High-SKU environments are harder to automate than low-SKU environments.
WMS (Warehouse Management System)Technology▼
Software managing all warehouse operations: receiving, slotting, pick path optimization, packing, and shipping. Leading platforms: Manhattan Associates, Blue Yonder, SAP EWM, HighJump. WMS integrates with ERP for inventory visibility and with TMS for carrier coordination.
TMS (Transportation Management System)Technology▼
Plans, executes, and optimizes freight movements. Key functions: load tendering to carriers, carrier selection and rate shopping, in-transit tracking, and freight audit and payment. Oracle TMS, BluJay (now E2open), MercuryGate, and Transplace are leading platforms.
Cross-DockingOperations▼
Product moves directly from inbound receiving dock to outbound shipping dock with minimal or no storage. Walmart pioneered cross-docking as a core supply chain strategy. Requires precise scheduling and supplier coordination. Reduces warehousing cost but demands execution discipline.
Cold ChainOperations▼
Temperature-controlled logistics for pharmaceutical products, food, and biologics. Requires specialized refrigerated trucks, temperature-monitored warehouses, and validated packaging. GDP (Good Distribution Practice) guidelines govern pharma cold chain. IoT sensors (Sensitech, Emerson) provide continuous monitoring.
Reverse LogisticsOperations▼
Returns processing and disposition: inspection, restocking, refurbishment, or disposal. $642B market in the US. Amazon returnless refund changed consumer expectations. Reverse logistics is costly — often $10-20 per return unit in labor and handling. Third-party returns specialists (Happy Returns, Optoro) are growing.
IntermodalOperations▼
Combining truck and rail for long-distance freight — truck handles first/last mile, rail handles the linehaul. J.B. Hunt pioneered intermodal as a lower-cost alternative to long-haul truckload. Typically 10-15% cheaper than TL but 1-2 days slower. Growing share of freight above 750 miles.
FOB (Free on Board)Trade▼
Incoterm defining when risk and ownership transfer from seller to buyer — at the origin port when goods are loaded. Contrast with DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) where seller bears all risk and cost to the destination. Incoterm selection affects who controls freight spend and carrier relationships.
Demurrage (Inland)Finance▼
Charges assessed for keeping a container at a DC or rail yard beyond the allotted free time. Distinct from port demurrage (charged at the port terminal). Inland demurrage costs surged during the 2021-22 supply chain crisis. Visibility tools help shippers proactively manage container returns.
Carrier Class / NMFCFinance▼
National Motor Freight Classification system assigns freight classes (50 to 500) used for LTL pricing. Classification is based on four factors: density, stowability, handling, and liability. Low-density, difficult-to-handle freight receives higher class numbers and higher rates per hundredweight.
OTD (On-Time Delivery)Metrics▼
Percentage of shipments delivered on the promised date. Primary carrier performance KPI in shipper contracts. Amazon targets greater than 99% OTD for its carrier network. Below-target OTD triggers carrier scorecarding and can result in volume reallocation or financial penalties.
Control TowerTechnology▼
Real-time visibility platform aggregating data from carriers, ports, and warehouses into a single view. Flexport, project44, and FourKites are leading platforms. Control towers use ML to predict ETAs and surface exceptions. Replaces reactive carrier check-calls with proactive exception management.
GreenMiles / Scope 3Sustainability▼
Logistics providers must now report Scope 3 emissions — carbon generated by customer supply chains that move through their networks. EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD) makes this mandatory for large companies. Shippers increasingly select carriers based on carbon intensity per tonne-km.

Common Workflows
Three end-to-end logistics workflows: inbound receiving, e-commerce fulfillment, and freight broker load execution.

Inbound Shipment to Warehouse Receipt

How goods move from a supplier purchase order to available inventory in a distribution center.
1
PO Creation and Supplier Booking
PO created in ERP and sent to supplier. Supplier books carrier (TL, LTL, or ocean) and sends ASN (Advance Ship Notice) to WMS with SKU-level packing details. WMS pre-builds expected receipt for faster receiving.
2
Carrier Arrival and Dock Check-In
Carrier arrives at receiving dock. Driver checks in via dock scheduling system (dock appointment required at high-volume DCs). WMS assigns dock door based on product type and put-away destination. Appointment compliance tracked to minimize detention charges.
3
Unloading and Receipt Scan
Associates unload and scan each carton or pallet. WMS compares scan results against ASN. Exceptions flagged: shortages, overages, wrong items, or visible damage. Exceptions create debit memo process against supplier. Photos captured for dispute resolution.
4
Put-Away Directed by WMS
WMS directs associates (or AMRs) to specific bin locations based on velocity slotting rules. Fast-moving SKUs slotted near outbound docks. Temperature-sensitive items directed to cold zones. License plate numbers (LPNs) track pallets through the building.
5
Inventory Available in ERP
On-hand inventory updated in ERP (SAP or Oracle) upon WMS confirmation of put-away. Inventory made available for order allocation. Safety stock and replenishment triggers evaluated. Cycle count schedule updated for new receipts.

E-Commerce Order Fulfillment (Pick-Pack-Ship)

How a consumer order flows from checkout to carrier pickup at the fulfillment center.
1
Order Received and Pick Wave Created
Order received from OMS (Shopify, Amazon, Salesforce Order Management). WMS creates pick wave batching orders by zone or carrier cutoff time. Wave planning optimizes pick paths to minimize travel. Rush orders flagged for priority wave.
2
Picking via RF Scanner or Automation
Picker directed via RF scanner to bin locations. Pick-to-light or voice-directed picking in high-velocity zones. AMR brings shelves to picker in goods-to-person configurations. Picks consolidated to tote or cart. Scan-verify at each pick ensures accuracy.
3
Packing Station Processing
Tote arrives at packing station. Associates verify items against pick list. Carton size selected to minimize dimensional weight. Packing slip inserted. Fragile items receive void fill. All items verified before seal — packing accuracy drives damage rate and return rate.
4
Label Print and Carrier Sort
Carrier label printed (UPS, FedEx, USPS, AMZL) based on service level and zone. Dimensional weight calculated; rate shopping selects lowest cost carrier meeting SLA. Cartons sorted to outbound dock lanes by carrier. Manifest generated for each carrier pickup.
5
Carrier Pickup and OTD Clock Starts
End-of-day carrier pickup at designated dock. Carrier scans manifest. Tracking number activated and sent to customer via OMS. OTD clock starts from carrier pickup. Control tower monitors in-transit status. Failed delivery attempt triggers redelivery workflow.

Freight Broker Load Execution

How a freight brokerage manages a single truckload shipment from tender to payment.
1
Shipper Submits Load Tender
Shipper submits load tender to broker: origin, destination, commodity, weight, requested pickup date, and target rate. TMS or EDI 204 transmits tender. Broker checks contracted carrier availability first before going to spot market.
2
Carrier Sourcing and Rate Negotiation
If contracted carrier unavailable or declines, broker posts to spot market via load board (DAT, Truckstop.com) or contacts carrier network directly. Rate negotiation based on market conditions, lane history, and carrier capacity. Digital brokers use automated matching algorithms to reduce sourcing time.
3
Carrier Acceptance and BOL Execution
Carrier accepts load. Rate confirmation transmitted. BOL (Bill of Lading) generated with commodity, weight, and special handling instructions. Pickup appointment confirmed with shipper. Carrier dispatch contacts driver with pickup details.
4
In-Transit Monitoring
TMS tracks load via ELD (electronic logging device) data or carrier check-calls. Broker monitors for delays and proactively notifies shipper. Appointment at destination confirmed. Any detention at origin tracked for potential billing. Control tower flags loads at risk of OTD miss.
5
Delivery, POD, and Settlement
Delivery completed. POD (Proof of Delivery) captured by driver and transmitted. Carrier submits invoice. Broker audits carrier invoice against agreed rate. Freight bill matched to BOL and POD. Carrier paid (typically net-30). Broker invoices shipper. Gross margin per load recorded.
Trends & Challenges
Six forces reshaping logistics economics, competitive dynamics, and technology adoption.
📦
Logistics Disintermediation by Amazon
Amazon Logistics now delivers 2.5B+ packages per year internally — volume that used to flow to UPS and FedEx. AMZL is now offering services to third-party merchants via Buy with Prime and Amazon Multi-Carrier Shipping Service. UPS and FedEx are being forced to compete for volume they previously owned, accelerating their pivot to healthcare and B2B verticals.
📡
Supply Chain Visibility as Table Stakes
project44 and FourKites now have $1B+ valuations. Shippers demand real-time ETAs and proactive exception alerts. Predictive ETA ML is replacing static schedules. Carrier electronic tracking acceptance rising from 30% to 80%+. Visibility is transitioning from differentiator to procurement requirement — shippers now include visibility SLAs in carrier contracts.
🛵
Last-Mile Economics Crisis
Last-mile is 50% of total logistics cost. Gig delivery (DoorDash Drive, Uber Direct) competing with traditional carriers for residential delivery. Density matters critically — urban delivery profitable at $8-12/stop, rural delivery often subsidized. EV fleet conversion (electric vans) is restructuring cost curves. UPS and FedEx residential surcharges are structural responses to this unit economics reality.
🤖
Warehouse Automation Surge
Labor costs plus availability are driving robotics adoption. Locus Robotics, AutoStore, and Geek+ are scaling deployments globally. Amazon Robotics (Kiva acquisition) set the benchmark — Amazon operates 750K+ robots across its network. Payback periods of 2-3 years for high-velocity DCs. AMRs are now common in 3PL facilities bidding on e-commerce fulfillment contracts.
🌎
Nearshoring & Supply Chain Restructuring
China+1 sourcing strategies are shifting freight patterns. Mexico nearshoring boom driven by USMCA advantages and reduced geopolitical risk. US-Mexico corridor freight is growing faster than transpacific lanes. Maersk is building Mexico distribution network. Industrial real estate in Monterrey and Juarez is at record occupancy. Rail (KCSM/CPKC) capacity into Mexico is constrained.
🌱
Sustainability & Carbon Reporting
EU CSRD makes Scope 3 logistics emissions mandatory for large companies. Green shipping corridors connecting major ports are being developed. Electric truck adoption accelerating (Freightliner eCascadia, Tesla Semi in commercial service). SAF (Sustainable Aviation Fuel) for air freight at 3-5x conventional jet fuel cost. Carbon labeling on shipments becoming a customer expectation in Europe.
Market Dynamics & Monetization
Pain points, monetization models, and competitive forces in global logistics.
Pain Points

Rate Volatility

Spot freight rates (trucking, ocean) swing 50-200% within a single year. 2021-22 ocean rates reached 10x normal levels. Shippers struggle to budget; carriers are reluctant to commit to long-term contracts. Risk management tools remain underdeveloped. Procurement teams increasingly use financial hedging instruments on ocean freight.

Capacity Fragmentation

US trucking has 500K+ carriers, 80% with fewer than 10 trucks. Digital brokers (Uber Freight, Convoy) attempted to aggregate carrier capacity at scale, but Convoy failed in 2023. Carrier relationship management is still largely manual. No single platform has achieved network-effect dominance in US trucking.

Last-Mile Profitability

Consumer expectations (free 2-day delivery) do not match the cost reality of $8-20 per residential delivery. UPS and FedEx residential surcharges are structural responses. Amazon subsidizes last-mile with Prime subscription revenue — a model pure-play carriers cannot replicate. Density (stops per mile) is the key lever for last-mile profitability.

Visibility Gaps

30-40% of ocean shipments still have no real-time tracking. Road freight tracking adoption is uneven across carrier segments. Multi-carrier shipments lose visibility at handoff points between carriers. Shippers are making replenishment and customer service decisions on stale data, leading to excess safety stock and poor exception management.

Labor & Driver Shortage

US truck driver shortage is 60K+ and growing due to aging workforce demographics. Warehouse labor turnover exceeds 100% annually at many facilities, driving 40-60% of fulfillment cost. Wage inflation is accelerating automation investment ROI calculations. H2B visa constraints limit seasonal fulfillment labor for holiday peak.

Market Structure
Top 10 logistics companies control less than 20% of the global market — this is an extremely fragmented industry. But digital platforms, scale economics, and M&A are driving consolidation. Maersk's integrator bet represents a $10B+ M&A wager on capturing a larger share of the logistics wallet versus accepting commoditization as a pure ocean carrier. CMA CGM (via CEVA), MSC (via Bolore acquisition), and Evergreen are pursuing similar vertical integration strategies. The strategic question: can ocean carriers build logistics businesses at scale, or will pure-play logistics specialists (DHL, UPS, XPO) defend their turf through operational excellence and customer relationships?

Historical Timeline
1800s
Railroad & Industrial Logistics
Railroads (1830s–1870s) were the first large-scale logistics networks; standardized gauge (1886); ICC (Interstate Commerce Commission, 1887) first freight rate regulation; railroad bill of lading standardizes shipment documentation; Sears Roebuck catalog (1893) proves mail-order fulfillment as a business model — the first DTC logistics operation.
1913
Ford Assembly Line & Scientific Management
Frederick Winslow Taylor "Principles of Scientific Management" (1911); Henry Ford River Rouge plant (1927) — first fully integrated supply chain; Ford owns mines, ships, railroads, and assembly plant; JIT thinking precursor; industrial logistics as a discipline distinct from transportation emerges.
1956
Air Freight & Containerization
Malcolm McLean's first container shipment (1956) transforms ocean freight costs; Boeing 707 (1958) enables air cargo at scale; Federal Express founded (Fred Smith, 1971) — overnight small parcel delivery; UPS expands from messenger service to national parcel carrier; modern parcel logistics born.
1970s–1980s
JIT & Deregulation
Toyota Production System / JIT manufacturing (Ohno, 1970s) pulls logistics into lean thinking; US trucking deregulation (Motor Carrier Act, 1980) — 18,000 new carriers enter market; airline deregulation (1978) enables FedEx's hub-spoke model; EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) automates purchase orders and ASNs; Walmart's cross-docking (1980s) sets supply chain efficiency standard.
1990s
ERP & Global Supply Chains
SAP R/3 (1992) integrates supply chain data across the enterprise; NAFTA (1994) expands North American supply chain integration; China WTO accession (2001) — offshore manufacturing explosion; Walmart's RFID mandate (2003); C.H. Robinson scales freight brokerage; the "China plus one" supply chain model is the default.
2000s
E-Commerce Logistics
Amazon Prime (2005) sets 2-day delivery expectation; Amazon Fulfillment Centers as logistics infrastructure; FedEx and UPS add residential surcharges; Zappos free shipping as competitive weapon; logistics becomes a consumer-facing brand differentiator, not just a back-office function; last-mile cost problem emerges.
2013–2019
Visibility & Platforms
Flexport founded (2013) as digital freight forwarder; project44 and FourKites launch real-time visibility (2014–2016); Uber Freight (2017) attempts to digitize trucking; Convoy (2015) raises $900M then fails (2023); DHL, UPS, FedEx race to build API-first logistics platforms; Amazon acquires Whole Foods (2017) expanding last-mile; Amazon AMZL internal delivery network scales.
2020–Present
COVID Resilience & Nearshoring
Pandemic disrupts global supply chains (2020–2022); port congestion costs $10B+; just-in-time gives way to just-in-case; nearshoring and friend-shoring accelerate; Mexico manufacturing boom (USMCA); Maersk acquires Senator International, LF Logistics — integrator strategy; warehouse robotics investment triples; AI demand forecasting becomes table stakes.
