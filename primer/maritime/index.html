Industry Overview
Maritime shipping carries approximately 90% of world trade by volume. Containerization — pioneered by Malcolm McLean in 1956 — revolutionized cargo handling, enabling global supply chains. Today, three alliances of mega-carriers control the majority of east-west container capacity, operating vessels exceeding 24,000 TEU.
📦

Container Shipping

Containerization revolutionized global trade. Top carriers Maersk, MSC, and CMA CGM control roughly 50% of global capacity. TEU (Twenty-foot Equivalent Unit) is the standard measure. Carrier alliances coordinate routes to optimize vessel utilization and port coverage.

⚓

Bulk Carriers

Dry bulk carries iron ore, coal, and grain in open holds. Tankers carry crude oil, LNG, and chemicals. VLCC (Very Large Crude Carrier) supertankers move up to 2M barrels. Vessel classes: Capesize (too large for Panama Canal), Panamax, and Handysize.

🏗️

Port Operations

Container terminals handle stevedoring, crane operations, and yard management. Largest ports: Shanghai, Singapore, Rotterdam, and Los Angeles/Long Beach. Port congestion was a critical global crisis in 2021-22, stalling supply chains and spiking freight costs.

🔧

Ship Management

Technical management covers crewing, maintenance, dry-docking, and flag state compliance. Third-party managers like Anglo-Eastern and V.Group operate vessels on behalf of owners. Separating ownership from operations is standard practice in the industry.

📋

Freight Forwarding & Logistics

NVOCCs and freight forwarders (Kuehne+Nagel, DB Schenker) arrange shipments without owning vessels. Services include customs brokerage and documentation — Bill of Lading (B/L) and Letter of Credit (LoC) are the core trade instruments.

💰

Maritime Finance & Insurance

Vessel financing via ship mortgages and sale-leaseback structures. P&I clubs (mutual insurers) cover third-party liability. Hull & Machinery insurance covers physical damage. Greek shipowners dominate tonnage ownership globally despite operating through international registries.

World's largest carrier by TEU capacity. Family-owned Swiss-Italian company. Rapidly expanded fleet through newbuilds and acquisitions. Now operates 700+ vessels.

CMA CGM

French carrier and world's 3rd largest. Owns CEVA Logistics for land-side integration. Family-controlled (Saade family). Aggressive digital and logistics acquisitions.

Container Alliance System

Three major alliances — 2M (Maersk+MSC, ending 2025), Ocean Alliance (CMA CGM+COSCO+Evergreen), and THE Alliance (Hapag-Lloyd+ONE+Yang Ming) — coordinate sailings and share vessel capacity.

Spot vs. Contract Rates

Shippers book at spot (current market) or annual contract rates. The Shanghai Containerized Freight Index (SCFI) benchmarks Asia-origin spot rates. Spread between spot and contract narrows in stable markets.

IMO 2050 Target

International Maritime Organization mandates net-zero greenhouse gas emissions from shipping by 2050. CII ratings grade vessels A-E annually. Methanol, ammonia, and LNG are the leading transitional fuel candidates.

Key Terminology
Essential vocabulary for maritime and container shipping — from TEUs and incoterms to port operations and decarbonization.
TEU (Twenty-foot Equivalent Unit)Core▼
The standard unit for measuring container capacity. One TEU equals one 20-foot shipping container. A 40-foot container equals 2 TEU. Vessel capacity, terminal throughput, and carrier rankings are all expressed in TEU. The largest vessels today carry 24,000+ TEU.
FCL / LCL (Full Container Load / Less than Container Load)Core▼
FCL means a shipper books an entire container for their cargo — faster and more secure. LCL means cargo from multiple shippers is consolidated into one container at a CFS (Container Freight Station). LCL is more economical for small shipments but slower due to consolidation and deconsolidation handling.
Bill of Lading (B/L)Documentation▼
The primary shipping document serving three functions: a receipt for cargo, a contract of carriage between shipper and carrier, and a document of title enabling transfer of ownership. The original B/L must be presented at destination to release cargo. Electronic Bills of Lading (eBL) are growing via platforms like WAVE and Bolero.
Dead FreightCommercial▼
A charge imposed on a shipper who books container space but fails to use it — similar to a hotel no-show fee. Dead freight compensates the carrier for the revenue lost from unused committed capacity. Common in contract arrangements with minimum quantity commitments (MQC).
Demurrage & DetentionCommercial▼
Demurrage is a charge for keeping a container at the port terminal beyond the carrier-allowed free time (typically 3-7 days). Detention is charged for keeping the container inland beyond free time after it leaves the port. Both became enormous issues during the 2021-22 port congestion crisis, with charges totaling billions across industry.
NVOCC (Non-Vessel Operating Common Carrier)Commercial▼
An entity that issues its own Bills of Lading and acts as a carrier to shippers, but arranges the actual ocean transport through vessel-operating carriers. NVOCCs aggregate cargo to negotiate better rates from carriers, then resell capacity to smaller shippers. Freight forwarders often operate as NVOCCs.
Freight Rate / All-In RateCommercial▼
The freight rate is the base cost to ship a container between two ports. The all-in rate includes the base ocean freight plus all applicable surcharges (BAF, CAF, port surcharges, terminal handling charges). Shippers compare all-in rates across carriers to evaluate true cost. Rates are quoted per TEU or per FEU (40-foot container).
BAF / CAF (Bunker Adjustment Factor / Currency Adjustment Factor)Commercial▼
BAF (also called Bunker Surcharge or FSC) is a fuel cost surcharge added to base freight rates, adjusting for fluctuations in bunker fuel prices. CAF adjusts for currency exchange rate movements in specific trade lanes. Both surcharges allow carriers to manage volatile cost inputs without renegotiating base rate contracts.
ETA / ETDOperations▼
Estimated Time of Arrival and Estimated Time of Departure — core schedule reliability metrics in container shipping. Schedule reliability industry-wide collapsed to 35-40% during 2021-22 port congestion. Carriers publish schedule reliability data; Maersk and Hapag-Lloyd consistently rank above industry average. Real-time ETA prediction via AIS data and ML is a key digital investment area.
Port CongestionOperations▼
A backlog of vessels waiting to berth at terminals, causing cascading delays and cost spikes across global supply chains. The 2021-22 congestion crisis at US West Coast ports (Los Angeles/Long Beach) was triggered by pandemic import surges overwhelming terminal capacity. At peak, 100+ vessels anchored offshore waiting for berths, costing shippers billions in demurrage and expedited freight alternatives.
Dry-DockingOperations▼
Mandatory maintenance performed with the vessel out of the water at a shipyard. Required every 2.5-5 years per classification society rules. Involves hull cleaning and painting, propeller inspection, structural surveys, and regulatory compliance checks. A vessel off-hire during dry-docking generates no revenue — scheduling and duration are critical cost management factors.
Flag State / Open RegistryRegulatory▼
The country under whose laws a vessel is registered. Open registries (Panama, Marshall Islands, Liberia) allow foreign owners to register vessels under their flag for lower costs and tax advantages. The flag state is responsible for enforcing international conventions (SOLAS, MARPOL) on registered vessels. Over 70% of world fleet tonnage flies flags of open registries.
IMO (International Maritime Organization)Regulatory▼
The United Nations specialized agency responsible for the safety, security, and environmental performance of international shipping. IMO sets global mandatory standards — SOLAS (safety), MARPOL (pollution prevention), and the 2050 net-zero decarbonization target. Member states enforce IMO conventions through their flag state administrations and port state control (PSC) inspections.
Incoterms (FOB, CIF, EXW, DDP)Documentation▼
International Commercial Terms published by the ICC defining the cost and risk transfer point between buyer and seller. FOB (Free On Board): seller delivers at origin port, buyer arranges ocean freight. CIF (Cost Insurance Freight): seller pays freight and insurance to destination port. EXW (Ex Works): buyer bears all costs from factory door. DDP (Delivered Duty Paid): seller responsible for full door-to-door delivery including duties.
SCFI (Shanghai Containerized Freight Index)Commercial▼
The benchmark spot freight rate index for container shipping published weekly by the Shanghai Shipping Exchange. Covers major trade lanes from Shanghai to Europe, US West Coast, US East Coast, Mediterranean, and others. SCFI is the primary market signal for container rate movements — spiked from ~1,000 in 2020 to 5,000+ in 2021-22, then collapsed back below 1,000 by 2023.
Slot Charter / Space CharterCommercial▼
An arrangement where one carrier purchases a specified number of container slots (spaces) on another carrier's vessel. Common within carrier alliances — partners share vessel capacity to cover more port pairs without each carrier deploying its own vessel on every string. Slot charters enable broader network coverage and improved vessel utilization.
Charter PartyCommercial▼
A contract for hiring a vessel. Time charter: charterer hires the vessel for a defined period, paying daily hire rate and covering fuel costs — used by carriers to supplement owned fleets. Voyage charter: charterer hires for a specific voyage, with the shipowner covering operating costs. Bareboat charter: charterer takes full operational control of the vessel.
GRI / PSS (General Rate Increase / Peak Season Surcharge)Commercial▼
Carrier rate adjustment mechanisms. GRI is a carrier-announced blanket rate increase applied across a trade lane, typically with 30 days notice. PSS is a temporary surcharge during high-demand periods (pre-Christmas, Chinese New Year). Shippers with contract rates may still face PSS on top of contract rates depending on contract terms and MQC fulfillment.
AIS (Automatic Identification System)Technology▼
A mandatory transponder system fitted on vessels over 300 GT that broadcasts position, speed, heading, and vessel identity. AIS data, collected by coastal stations and satellites (S-AIS), is the foundation of vessel tracking platforms. Aggregated AIS data powers cargo intelligence firms (Kpler, Windward) and ETA prediction models across the maritime industry.
Carbon Intensity Indicator (CII)Regulatory▼
An IMO mandatory annual vessel decarbonization rating (A through E) that measures carbon intensity as CO2 emitted per cargo-carrying capacity per nautical mile. Vessels rated D or E for three consecutive years must implement corrective action plans. CII is driving fleet upgrade investments, slow steaming practices, and accelerated scrapping of inefficient older vessels.

Common Workflows
Core operational processes — from booking a container to negotiating annual freight contracts.

Container Booking to Vessel Load

How a shipper gets cargo from factory floor to vessel departure — the end-to-end booking and documentation workflow.
1
Shipper Requests Quote
Shipper contacts freight forwarder or books directly via carrier portal or platform (Freightos, Maersk.com). Provides: origin, destination, commodity, weight, volume, and required date. Forwarder checks contract rates and spot market, returns all-in rate including surcharges.
2
Freight Forwarder Books Space with Carrier
Forwarder (as NVOCC) or shipper directly places booking with carrier system (e.g., Inttra, direct API). Carrier confirms slot on a specific vessel and voyage. Container equipment (dry, reefer, or special) is reserved. Booking confirmation number issued with vessel name, voyage, and port rotation.
3
VGM / SI Cutoff — Cargo Instructions Submitted
Shipper submits Shipping Instructions (SI) — cargo details, marks, consignee, and notify party for B/L generation. Verified Gross Mass (VGM) declaration submitted per SOLAS mandate. Cutoffs typically 48-72 hours before vessel departure. Late submissions risk missing the vessel.
4
Terminal Receiving and Stuffing
Empty container delivered to shipper or cargo brought to container freight station (for LCL). Cargo stuffed and sealed. Container gated into terminal — gate-in transaction recorded in TOS. Terminal assigns stack position in yard. Dangerous goods require advance approval and special stowage.
5
Vessel Load and Departure Confirmation with B/L Issued
Terminal loads container onto vessel per bay plan (optimized for weight, stability, and port rotation sequence). After vessel departure, carrier issues original Bill of Lading (or express release for trusted shippers). Draft B/L reviewed by shipper for accuracy before surrender. Shipper uses B/L to trigger payment under Letter of Credit.

Port Call Operations — Container Terminal

What happens from the moment a vessel arrives at port to when it departs — the terminal operations sequence.
1
Vessel Arrival and Pilot Boarding
Vessel notifies port authority of arrival (Notice of Readiness). Marine pilot boards vessel outside port limits to guide into berth. Port authority coordinates VTS (Vessel Traffic Service). Berth allocation confirmed or vessel anchors if berth unavailable — anchor waiting time is off-hire and causes schedule failures.
2
Berth Allocation and Crane Gang Assignment
Terminal allocates berth window based on vessel arrival, LOA (length overall), and draft. Stevedore crane gangs (typically 3-6 cranes per ULCV) assigned. Pre-planned discharge and load sequence loaded into terminal operating system. Container yard positions pre-allocated to minimize horizontal transport distance.
3
Discharge and Load Sequencing by Bay Plan
Ship planner optimizes bay plan for stability, weight distribution, and port rotation (containers for next port loaded last / discharged first). Shore-based cranes discharge import containers and load export containers simultaneously. Quay crane productivity (moves/hour) directly determines vessel turnaround time. Reefer containers monitored continuously for temperature.
4
Customs Examination of Flagged Containers
Customs authority uses manifest data, risk profiling, and scanning (X-ray or gamma) to flag containers for physical examination. 2-5% of containers typically examined at major ports. Holds delay cargo release and can miss vessel connections. Pre-arrival filing (e.g., 24-hour rule in US) allows pre-clearance to minimize delays.
5
Vessel Departure and Manifest Transmission
Terminal sends final load list to carrier confirming all loaded containers, weights, and positions. Carrier transmits departure manifest to next port customs authority. Port authority clears vessel for departure. Pilot escorts vessel out of port limits. Terminal records actual moves and calculates productivity KPIs vs. plan.

Freight Rate Negotiation Cycle

How shippers and carriers negotiate annual contract rates — the commercial cycle that governs most large-volume cargo.
1
Annual Contract Tender Launched by Shipper
Large shippers (retailers, manufacturers, BCOs) launch RFP process typically Oct-Jan for service starting April 1 (trans-Pacific contract year). RFP defines trade lanes, volume commitments, cargo specifications, service requirements (transit time, schedule reliability), and evaluation criteria. Benchmarking data from Xeneta and Drewry used to establish market reference rates.
2
Carriers Submit RFP Bids
Carrier commercial teams respond with rate bids per trade lane, proposed service string, and minimum quantity commitment (MQC) requirements. Larger shippers receive more aggressive pricing in exchange for volume commitment. Carriers balance rate competitiveness against vessel utilization targets and alliance slot obligations.
3
Benchmarking vs. SCFI / Xeneta Market Data
Shipper procurement team benchmarks submitted bids against SCFI spot index, Xeneta rate intelligence (actual market rates paid), and peer network intelligence. Carriers offering rates significantly above market are challenged or eliminated. Shipper evaluates rate vs. service quality (reliability, equipment availability, transit time) — not rate alone.
4
Contract Awarded with MQC
Shipper awards contracts to 2-4 carriers per major trade lane to maintain competition and backup capacity. Service contract (SC) filed with FMC for US trades. MQC (Minimum Quantity Commitment) agreed — typically 70-80% of forecasted volume. Failure to meet MQC triggers dead freight charges. Rate effective date, surcharge treatment, and GRI/PSS clauses negotiated.
5
Mid-Year GRI Negotiations if Spot Market Spikes
If spot market rises sharply above contract rates (as in 2021), carriers invoke GRI or PSS clauses. Shippers with strong MQC fulfillment and long-term relationships negotiate to cap or reject surcharges. Carriers facing equipment shortages may de-prioritize contract shippers below MQC. Rate collapse (2022-23) reversed dynamic — carriers desperate to retain any contract volume.
Trends & Challenges
Forces reshaping ocean freight, port operations, and maritime supply chains globally.
🌿
Decarbonization & Green Fuels
IMO 2050 net-zero target is driving the biggest technology transition in shipping history. Methanol, ammonia, and LNG are leading transitional fuel candidates — each with infrastructure and cost challenges. Maersk ordered the industry's first methanol-powered vessels. CII ratings create annual pressure on fleet operators. Green shipping corridors between major ports are accelerating adoption.
💻
Digital Freight Platforms
Freightos and Flexport are challenging the traditional freight broker model with real-time rate transparency and API-driven booking. However, roughly 80% of bookings still happen via email and phone. Carrier direct-booking portals (Maersk.com) aim to disintermediate forwarders. The gap between digital aspiration and operational reality remains wide across most of the industry.
🔗
Supply Chain Resilience Post-2021
The 2021-22 pandemic disruptions exposed the fragility of just-in-time supply chains. Port congestion at US West Coast ports cost the global economy $10B+. Carriers earned record profits ($150B+ industry in 2022) followed by a collapse as demand normalized. Shippers are diversifying sourcing, nearshoring production, and building inventory buffers as a structural response.
🤝
Alliance Consolidation & Antitrust
Three major alliances control approximately 80% of east-west capacity. The 2M alliance (Maersk+MSC) split in 2025 after 10 years, with both carriers going independent. Regulatory scrutiny is increasing — US FMC, EU DG COMP, and China SAMR all investigating alliance practices, surcharge transparency, and anti-competitive coordination.
🤖
Autonomous & Remote Vessels
Yara Birkeland launched as the world's first autonomous electric container feeder in Norway. Remote operations centers allow shore-based teams to monitor and assist vessel navigation. Full autonomy on international trade routes remains years away — regulatory frameworks under IMO are still nascent. The near-term focus is decision-support AI rather than full replacement of bridge crews.
⚠️
Red Sea & Geopolitical Risk
Houthi attacks in the Red Sea from late 2023 rerouted approximately $200B/month of trade via the Cape of Good Hope, adding 10-14 days transit time and spiking freight rates 200-400% in early 2024. The crisis demonstrated shipping's extreme vulnerability to geopolitical chokepoints. Suez Canal, Strait of Malacca, and Panama Canal (drought-affected 2023-24) all represent systemic concentration risk for global trade.
Market Dynamics & Monetization
Pain points, monetization models, and competitive forces in maritime and ocean freight.
📈

Rate Cycle Volatility

Ocean freight spot rates swung from $1,500/TEU (pre-COVID) to $20,000+/TEU (2021 peak) and back to $1,200/TEU (2023 trough). Carriers earned record profits ($150B+ industry-wide in 2021–22), then near-losses in 2023. Shippers locked in long-term contracts lost when spot rates fell. Carriers that over-ordered vessels during the boom face overcapacity through 2025–26.

🔗

Alliance Concentration Risk

Three alliances (2M, Ocean Alliance, THE Alliance) coordinate vessel sharing across 80% of east-west capacity. Maersk and MSC split the 2M alliance in 2025. New alliance formation creates uncertainty for shippers on schedules and service reliability. Regulatory scrutiny of alliances is growing — DOJ and EU are examining coordination practices that would be illegal in other industries.

🌍

Geopolitical Route Disruption

Houthi attacks in the Red Sea (2024) forced 90% of Asia-Europe trade to reroute via Cape of Good Hope — adding 14 days and $1,000+/TEU in cost. Panama Canal drought (2023) restricted drafts and caused vessel queuing. Suez blockage (2021, Ever Given) cost $9B/day. Maritime supply chains are exposed to geopolitical events that cannot be hedged or predicted.

🌿

Decarbonization Cost Burden

IMO 2050 net-zero target requires fundamental fuel transition. Green methanol vessels cost 20–30% more than conventional; green ammonia bunkering infrastructure barely exists. CII ratings (A–E) penalize high-emission vessels with port restrictions. EU Emissions Trading System now covers maritime. The $1–3T industry-wide decarbonization cost over 25 years falls primarily on carriers and charterers — with no carbon premium yet priced into freight rates.

📋

Demurrage & Detention Revenue Controversy

Carriers earned $6–12B annually in demurrage and detention fees during 2021–22 congestion crisis. FMC (Federal Maritime Commission) investigated carrier practices, finding carriers used congestion as excuse to charge fees even when shippers had no control over delays. New FMC rules (2024) require carriers to demonstrate container availability before charging detention. D&D has shifted from incidental to structural revenue — a politically unstable monetization strategy.

Evolution & Foundations
How ocean shipping evolved from Phoenician trade routes to a $500B+ industry moving 90% of world trade — the key inflection points, technological shifts, and foundational works that define modern maritime logistics.
3000 BC–1400s

Ancient Trade Routes

Phoenician merchant ships (1000 BC) establish Mediterranean trade networks; Chinese junks reach East Africa (1400s, Zheng He); Venetian spice trade dominance; shipping as civilization's original supply chain; the concept of a bill of lading exists in ancient Mesopotamian clay tablets; Lloyd's of London precursor risks are Mediterranean cargo.

1600s–1800s

Age of Sail & Insurance

British East India Company (1600) and VOC (Dutch, 1602) — first corporate shipping empires; Lloyd's of London formalizes marine insurance (1688–1774); clipper ship era; steam power (Savannah, first steam-assisted Atlantic crossing 1819); iron hull replaces wood; Suez Canal opens (1869) — cuts London-Bombay route by 43 days.

1956

The Container Revolution

Malcolm McLean loads 58 metal containers onto SS Ideal X (Newark, April 26, 1956) — first containerized cargo shipment; standardized 20-foot equivalent unit (TEU) concept; ISO standardizes container dimensions (1961, 1968); container shipping reduces cargo handling costs 90%+; transforms global trade economics irreversibly.

1960s–1970s

Scale & Alliances

First VLCC (Very Large Crude Carrier) supertankers; containerships grow from 500 TEU to 3,000 TEU; UNCTAD Code of Conduct for Liner Conferences (1974); conference system (price-fixing cartels) dominate ocean freight; SOLAS (Safety of Life at Sea) convention revisions; IMO established as UN agency (1948, operational 1958).

1984–1990s

Deregulation & Digital

US Shipping Act (1984) deregulates ocean freight; conference system weakens; EDI (Electronic Data Interchange) for B/L and manifest; AIS (Automatic Identification System) development; container ships reach 6,000 TEU (1990s); Maersk, P&O, Sealand begin consolidation; global alliances (Grand Alliance 1996) form.

2008–2012

Financial Crisis & Overcapacity

Orderbook from 2006–2008 boom delivers into post-crisis demand slump; containership rates collapse; carriers lose $20B+ (2009–2012); Hanjin Shipping bankruptcy (2016) largest maritime insolvency; SCFI index launched (2009) as spot rate benchmark; carrier consolidation accelerates; Top 10 carriers control 85% of capacity by 2020.

2020–2022

COVID Supply Chain Crisis

Pandemic demand surge meets port congestion and equipment shortage; SCFI Shanghai-LA rates from $1,500 to $20,000/FEU; carriers earn $150B+ profit over 2 years; blank sailings manipulate capacity; demurrage/detention fees become $10B+ revenue stream; FMC investigates carrier practices; new vessel orders surge (to be delivered 2023–2025).

2023–Present

Rate Collapse & Red Sea

2023 rate crash — oversupply from pandemic orderbook + demand normalization; Houthi Red Sea attacks (Nov 2023) force rerouting via Cape; rates spike 200–400% again; IMO 2050 net-zero commitment drives methanol/ammonia vessel orders; Maersk first methanol vessel delivery; EU ETS maritime (2024); CII ratings begin affecting vessel commercial value.
