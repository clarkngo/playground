Context — NCyTE Center
The NCyTE Center (National Cybersecurity Teaching Excellence Center) is an NSF-funded Advanced Technological Education (ATE) center focused on cybersecurity education at community and technical colleges. It develops curricula, provides professional development for educators, and serves as a national resource for cybersecurity education programs. The fact that this workshop was hosted at CityU signals an active relationship between NCyTE and the institution.

This two-day professional development workshop targeted educators — primarily from cybersecurity and technology programs — who wanted practical skills for integrating AI into their teaching. The curriculum was delivered through the Canvas LMS and covered everything from AI fundamentals and LLM comparison to instructional design, ethics, and interactive tool creation.

The workshop had a strong hands-on emphasis: nearly every session included a live activity. Participants built lesson plans, compared LLMs side by side, designed chatbots, created AI-generated multimedia content, and drafted AI use policies. The Canvas course remained accessible after the workshop for continued reference.

Context — CISM and CMMC
Two certification frameworks surfaced in the cybersecurity context: CISM (Certified Information Security Manager) is an ISACA credential for security management professionals covering governance, risk, program development, and incident response. CMMC (Cybersecurity Maturity Model Certification) is a DoD framework requiring defense contractors to meet specific cybersecurity standards across five maturity levels. Both represent real-world credential targets relevant to students in cybersecurity programs.

03 Key Concepts
CO-STAR Prompting Framework
A structured approach to writing effective AI prompts. Each letter represents a layer of context that helps the model produce more accurate, useful output.
 
Element
What it does
C
Context
Background information the AI needs to understand your situation
O
Objective
The specific task or goal you want the AI to accomplish
S
Style
Writing style: formal, casual, academic, conversational
T
Tone
Emotional register: authoritative, empathetic, encouraging, neutral
A
Audience
Who the output is for: students, executives, beginners, experts
R
Response
Format of the output: bullet list, essay, table, JSON, slide deck
The principle behind CO-STAR: the more specific the prompt, the better the output. Vague prompts produce generic results; structured prompts produce targeted, usable content.
The Grade Weight Flip
One of the most concrete and immediately actionable ideas from the workshop. Rather than trying to detect or prevent AI use, restructure what assessments are worth so that AI-resistant activities carry the most weight.
Before
Final exam: 60%
Project: 10%
After
Final exam: 10%
Project: 60%
The logic: a final exam is easy to cheat with AI. A multi-week project that shows a student’s process, decisions, and iteration is much harder to fake entirely. Reducing the final exam to 10% removes most of the incentive for academic dishonesty on that particular artifact.
AI Detection: Perplexity and Burstiness
Tools like Turnitin use two statistical signals to flag AI-generated text:

Perplexity measures how predictable the word choices are. AI models optimize for the most likely next word, producing text that flows very smoothly — almost too smoothly. Human writing makes unexpected word choices and takes detours that feel natural but are statistically surprising. Low perplexity = AI-like.

Burstiness measures variation in sentence length and complexity. Humans naturally write in bursts: a short punchy sentence, then a longer more complex one, then another short one. AI tends to produce uniform sentence lengths with consistent complexity throughout. Low burstiness = AI-like.

The arms race implication: students who know these signals can use AI "humanizer" tools to artificially increase perplexity and burstiness, making AI text look more human. This is why detection is an unreliable long-term strategy — the grade weight flip addresses the root problem instead.
Digital Provenance and C2PA
Digital provenance refers to the verifiable chain of custody for a digital artifact: who created it, when, with what tools, and what modifications were made. As AI-generated images, audio, and text become indistinguishable from human-created content, provenance becomes essential for trust.

The C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity) is an open technical standard that cryptographically signs metadata into content at the point of creation. Major adopters include Adobe, Microsoft, Google, and camera manufacturers. The IPTC Photo Metadata Standard is a related framework specifically for photographic content.

For educators: understanding digital provenance is part of AI literacy. Students who consume and create digital content need to know that origin can be tracked, verified, or forged — and what the implications are for academic work, journalism, and cybersecurity.
Dynamic Assessment: AI-Generated Quizzes and Case Studies
Several ideas clustered around making assessments more responsive and relevant:

• Dynamically generated quizzes via API or from learning objectives — each student gets a slightly different quiz generated on demand, making answer-sharing less useful
• AI-updated case studies — the scenario is refreshed with current events or new data, keeping the case study relevant and harder to reuse from previous semesters
• Role-play prompting — "You are a cybersecurity manager facing a ransomware incident; advise your executive team" — puts students in authentic professional scenarios
• Validation with two AIs — submit the same question to two different models and compare results; trains critical evaluation of AI output
• QTI generation — AI can produce quizzes in the QTI (Question and Test Interoperability) XML format, which Canvas can import directly for bulk question creation
"Academia’s Customer Is the Job Market; Students Are the Product"
A deliberately provocative reframing offered during the workshop. It inverts the usual language of student-centeredness to surface a structural truth: higher education’s ultimate output is graduates who are employable, and the people most invested in the quality of that output are employers — not the students themselves (who pay tuition) and not the institution (which confers credentials).

The framing is uncomfortable because it treats students as means rather than ends. But it is useful for forcing clarity on questions like: what does a successful graduate actually look like? Who gets to define that? And how do we know when we’ve produced one?
AI Policy: A Three-Tier Framework
A practical policy structure introduced for classroom AI governance — parallel to Kristin Copeland’s traffic-light system from the Higher Education Summit 2026:

• AI OK — Use AI freely (example: building a PowerPoint deck)
• AI in some parts — Use AI for ideation, brainstorming, or expanding an idea, but not for the full deliverable (example: project planning phase, not the final report)
• AI not OK — No AI; the assessment is designed to measure unassisted ability (example: final exam)

This structure pairs naturally with the grade weight flip: if the final exam is only 10% of the grade and is an "AI not OK" zone, the stakes of that restriction are manageable. Most of the grade comes from project work where AI assistance is expected and visible.
NotebookLM: Study Guides, Podcasts, and Interactive Audio
Google’s NotebookLM allows users to upload documents (PDFs, slides, notes, URLs) and interact with them via chat. Its standout feature for education is the Audio Overview: it generates a realistic two-host podcast-style discussion of the uploaded content. The "join the podcast" mode allows real-time interruption — you can ask the AI hosts questions and they respond in character, mid-discussion.

Use cases: students generate study guide podcasts from their own notes; faculty create audio supplements to readings; learners can ask follow-up questions in a low-stakes conversational format. Particularly valuable for auditory learners and students who struggle with dense reading.

Context — QTI
QTI (Question and Test Interoperability) is an XML-based standard maintained by 1EdTech (formerly IMS Global) for representing quiz and test questions in a portable format. Canvas supports QTI import, allowing bulk quiz creation from AI-generated question sets without manually entering each question. This is a significant time-saver for faculty building assessments at scale.

06 Synthesis
Theme 1
Structure the Assessment, Not the Detection
The grade weight flip is the workshop’s most powerful idea. Spending effort detecting AI use is a losing arms race. Restructuring what assessments are worth — so that high-weight assessments are process-visible and AI-resistant by design — removes the incentive rather than trying to catch the behavior. The three-tier AI policy (OK / partial / not OK) gives this structure a communication framework.
Theme 2
Specificity Is the Core Skill
The CO-STAR framework, the emphasis on domain-specific prompts, and the principle that "the more specific, the better" all point to the same underlying skill: the ability to articulate precisely what you need. This is a transferable cognitive skill — not just AI literacy. Students who learn to write clear, structured prompts are also learning to think clearly about goals, audience, and constraints.
Theme 3
AI as Production Environment, Not Just a Tool
The workshop treated AI as infrastructure for course design: generating quizzes dynamically, updating case studies, building chatbots, creating interactive tools, producing podcasts, and drafting policies. This framing — AI as the production layer of teaching, not just a convenience — has significant implications for faculty workload and for how institutions should support course development.
Theme 4
Dual-AI Validation Is Converging as a Best Practice
Both this workshop and the Higher Education Summit 2026 independently surfaced the practice of running the same query through two or more AI models and comparing results. The convergence from two separate professional development sources in the same period suggests this is becoming a recognized best practice for AI-literate educators — and should be formalized as a teachable exercise.
Theme 5
Ethics Is Technical, Not Just Philosophical
Module 7 treated AI ethics as a technical domain: perplexity and burstiness are measurable signals; C2PA is an implementable standard; digital provenance is an engineering problem. This grounds ethics in the specific, verifiable, and actionable — a more useful frame for technology educators than abstract principle-listing.
07 My Own Thoughts
Thought 01
The Grade Weight Flip Is the Most Structurally Honest Response to AI
Every other response to AI in assessments — detection tools, honor codes, surveillance — is adversarial. The grade weight flip is not. It says: we accept that AI exists, we accept that students will use it, and we are designing the assessment to measure what actually matters (process, iteration, judgment) rather than what AI can trivially produce (a polished final document). This is the most intellectually honest position I have seen in two workshops and one summit on this topic.
Thought 02
"Academia’s Customer Is the Job Market" Is Uncomfortable Because It’s Partially True
The framing is reductive — education has intrinsic value beyond employability. But it is useful precisely because it is uncomfortable. If we take it seriously, it forces the question: are our graduates actually prepared for what employers want? And if not, who is responsible? The Higher Ed Summit surfaced the same tension: employer co-design, demand signals, and workforce alignment all assume that the job market has a legitimate claim on curriculum design. This framing just makes that assumption explicit.
Thought 03
CO-STAR Should Be Taught as a Thinking Framework, Not Just a Prompt Template
The value of CO-STAR is not that it produces better AI outputs (though it does). It is that it forces the prompter to think through their actual goal before interacting with the AI. Most bad prompts fail not because of poor syntax but because the person had not yet clarified what they actually wanted. CO-STAR is a pre-thinking tool. Teaching it as a prompting technique undersells it; teaching it as a planning skill is more accurate.
Thought 04
Both Workshops Converged on Dual-AI Validation Independently
This workshop introduced "validation with 2 AIs." The Higher Education Summit had the multi-AI comparison as a class exercise. Neither referenced the other. The independent convergence is worth noting: when two separate communities of practice arrive at the same practice without coordination, it usually means the practice is pointing at something real. This should be a standard, named exercise in AI literacy curricula — not an advanced elective.
Thought 05
NotebookLM’s Join-the-Podcast Feature Is Underrated
The ability to interrupt a podcast and ask the AI hosts questions in real time is a genuinely different learning modality. It combines passive listening with active inquiry in a low-stakes, conversational format. For students who are intimidated by asking questions in class, or who process information better through audio, this could be transformative. The question is whether faculty will invest the time to create good source material for it, since the output quality depends heavily on what you upload.
