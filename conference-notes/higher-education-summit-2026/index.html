01 Event Overview
Date
April 9, 2026
Hours
8:30 AM – 5:00 PM
Venue
Seattle Colleges – Georgetown Campus (South Seattle Community College)
Host
Dr. Kathy Cox, Higher Education Leader

The Higher Education Summit 2026 brought together faculty, administrators, workforce development professionals, industry partners, and students from across Washington State to examine AI's growing role in higher education. The event featured a keynote address, two moderated panels, three structured breakout sessions, and a closing community discussion.

The summit's overarching goal was to generate a shared AI in Higher Education Playbook — a practical roadmap covering institutional readiness, responsible AI integration, and alignment with future workforce needs. Each breakout session was designed to contribute a section to that collective document.

Attendees represented a wide range of institution types: large research universities, community colleges, technical colleges, and workforce-focused nonprofits. This mix created productive friction between institutions with significant resources (Arizona State University) and those operating with tighter constraints (Clover Park Technical College, South Seattle College, Highline College).

Context
Washington State has a robust community college system (34 colleges under the State Board for Community and Technical Colleges, SBCTC) that serves as the primary gateway to workforce training for a large portion of the state's population. Programs like I-BEST (Integrated Basic Education and Skills Training) — co-taught by a basic skills instructor and a technical instructor — are nationally recognized models for helping English learners and under-prepared adults enter career pathways. These community colleges operate under significantly different resource constraints than four-year universities, a tension that surfaced repeatedly throughout the day.
02 Networking & Notable Contacts

Aetheon (aetheon.ai) — an AI company focused on gamified assessment — was one of the notable organizations represented at the event.

Conversation with James Bowen — Aetheon
James and I discussed gamified assessment and the challenge of AI bias, specifically implicit bias in how AI systems evaluate learners. The conversation raised the question of whether AI assessment tools can be culturally neutral, or whether the data they are trained on encodes assumptions about what "correct" looks like.
My Thought — Dual-AI Cultural Simulation
What if we trained one AI model on one cultural context — its language, social norms, history, and values — and another on a different context entirely? Then we placed them both into the same scenario and observed how they responded. This could be a powerful way to surface implicit bias in AI behavior and to make abstract concepts like cultural bias concrete and visible for students. It could also be a useful classroom exercise in AI literacy. See the expanded version in My Own Thoughts.

A recurring undercurrent throughout the summit was the divide between AI-enthusiast and AI-skeptic faculty. Even within the same institution, opinions ranged from enthusiastic early adoption to principled resistance. Both perspectives were treated as legitimate inputs to the conversation rather than obstacles to overcome.

03 Opening Remarks — Dr. Kathy Cox
Dr. Kathy Cox — Higher Education Leader & Summit Organizer

Dr. Cox opened the summit with a clear statement of purpose: give Washingtonians the best possible education, and move beyond outdated assessment models like blue-book quizzes. She framed the day not as a debate about whether AI matters, but as a working session to figure out what to do about it.

60%
of jobs globally will require significant upskilling by 2027, according to the World Economic Forum’s Future of Jobs Report 2023. This figure set the urgency for the day’s conversations.

The breakout sessions were designed to be generative, not just reflective. Each session would contribute content to a shared AI in Higher Education Playbook — a living document to serve as a roadmap for institutions across Washington.

04 Keynote — Mike Meotti, WSAC
Mike Meotti — Executive Director, Washington Student Achievement Council (WSAC)
Context
The Washington Student Achievement Council (WSAC) is a state agency focused on expanding access to and affordability of higher education in Washington. It does not have regulatory authority over AI use in institutions — a point Meotti made explicit at the outset. There is currently no official state-level position on AI in education.

Meotti opened with a series of quick audience polls designed to calibrate the room and reveal gaps in awareness:

QuestionResult
Who has read Ethan Mollick?~10%
Who uses a paid AI model?~90%
Who uses social media where AI bots operate freely?~5 hands (Moltbot / Openclaw)
Which AI company is considered the most safety-conscious?~5% answered correctly: Anthropic
Who knows what a Mimeograph machine is?~5% (icebreaker)
Context
The Mimeograph was a mechanical duplicating machine invented in the 1880s, widely used in schools and offices through the 1980s before being replaced by photocopiers. Meotti used it as an icebreaker to make the point that every disruptive technology eventually becomes invisible infrastructure — and that we tend to forget how disruptive it felt at first.

Key arguments from the keynote:

On humility and industry skepticism
Do not trust statements made by people in the AI industry at face value. The press amplifies claims — both fears and promises — just as they did with Y2K. Be critical. AI is too important to be understood only through the lens of those building it.
On technology and inequality
Technology innovation and Wall Street have together widened the socioeconomic gap. Higher education cannot afford to repeat the pattern of ignoring who gets left behind — as happened with blue-collar displacement in earlier decades.
On the role of higher education
"Technology is too important to be left to the technology companies." The responsibility of higher education is to help all students learn how to operate in this new environment — not necessarily to teach them how to code, but to develop informed, critical, capable people.
On reciprocal transparency
Faculty should tell students when they use AI. Students should tell faculty when they use AI. Meotti called this reciprocal transparency — a norm rather than a rule, but one that builds the trust necessary for honest conversations about learning.
On the best analogy for AI
The right comparison for AI is not the introduction of the cell phone, nor the graphical user interface. It is closer to how humans domesticated animals. We did not simply adopt a tool — we brought something into our world that had its own behaviors, instincts, and eventually its own role in our lives. AI is already showing that it can act autonomously. That changes the nature of the relationship.
On Dario Amodei and Anthropic
Meotti encouraged attendees to read what Dario Amodei (CEO and co-founder of Anthropic) has written directly — not filtered through press coverage. He noted that some of the most thoughtful public writing on AI risk and AI potential is coming from people inside the frontier labs, but it requires going to the source.

Books referenced:

05 Panel 1: Workforce
Emily Parkhurst — CEO, Formidable Media (Moderator)
Ingrid Stegemoeller — Industry Engagement Director, Partnership for Learning
Dr. Lauren Van Koughnett — Associate VP, We Are Washington Employers
Libby Johnson McKee — CEO, Goodwill Industries
Ingrid Stegemoeller — on what employers want
Employers across all sectors consistently cite people skills as the most critical quality in job candidates. It is a universal need — not specific to any industry or role level.
My Thought — The Soft Skills Paradox
We often hear that soft skills are the most critical asset in the modern workforce. But much of the technology we build and use is specifically designed to remove "human friction" — replacing conversations with tickets, meetings with pings, phone calls with chatbots. Is the tech industry inadvertently atrophying the very people skills it claims to value most?
Libby Johnson McKee — on Goodwill and AI
Goodwill is the largest provider of free education and workforce services in the region. They are approaching AI as beginners — starting from scratch — and are looking for a growth mindset in everyone they work with, including their own staff. The willingness to learn matters more than existing AI knowledge.
Dr. Lauren Van Koughnett — on tool preference and human judgment
Their organization prefers Claude and Perplexity over ChatGPT for most tasks. However, when a student fell from a school building — a real crisis requiring human connection — AI was not the right tool. The situation required authentic relationship skills, not language model output.
My Thought — AI in Conflict
If human emotion and relationship skills are what we need in a crisis — but human emotion also drives conflicts, wars, and escalation — is AI’s emotional neutrality sometimes an asset? Or does removing guilt and empathy from a decision-making process just make it easier to stay in conflict indefinitely?
Libby Johnson McKee — on bad prompting and equity
She has observed workers using AI with poor prompts and getting poor results. Critical thinking is essential, especially for workers whose first language is not English or who are unfamiliar with U.S. workplace norms. AI could help bridge that gap — but only if people know how to use it effectively.
Ingrid Stegemoeller — on curriculum relevance
Her husband, a teacher, brings up critical thinking as a daily concern. AI makes it effortless for students to generate a five-paragraph essay without engaging in the thinking the essay was supposed to develop. She is not an AI expert — she is a workforce expert — and she emphasizes employer co-design as the essential ingredient in any program that works. Employers want to participate in building quality workforce learning, not just receive graduates. Programs that allow on-the-job training embedded in the schedule have seen the most success.
Dr. Lauren Van Koughnett — on equity and pace
Speed is the enemy of equity. Smaller institutions that lack social and technological capital will inevitably lag behind larger, better-resourced universities. Multi-institutional programs are one of the most effective responses to this gap — they allow smaller colleges to share infrastructure and expertise they could not build alone. Programs co-designed with employers in the first quarter of development are consistently more successful than those designed in isolation.
Libby Johnson McKee — on digital exclusion
Some of the people Goodwill serves have never used a computer mouse. Others live in parts of the world that lack consistent electricity. The pace of AI change is deeply concerning for anyone already behind in the digital economy. Cross-sector dialogue is not optional — it is the minimum requirement for not leaving people behind.
Are there unique skills higher education should address beyond AI?
Ingrid: Federal and state policy requirements create a very long list of things institutions are expected to do. Educators are often in reactive mode, catching up to what industries actually need. There is no common vocabulary between education and industry for defining skill needs. Better "demand signals" from employers would help institutions prioritize and align faster.
Microsoft now reports that 30% of its code is written by AI. How quickly should curricula be updated?
Ingrid: The pace of update needs to match the pace of change in each specific sector. The tech sector and the maritime sector have entirely different timelines. Some industries are simultaneously managing an AI transition and a large wave of retirements from experienced workers.
Where are the hiccups and opportunities in collaboration between workforce partners and universities?
Libby: Tasks are being replaced, not entire jobs — but we do not know what those jobs will look like in six months. Companies are not flush with cash and are affected by tariffs and economic pressure. AI and automation have still helped operations grow. The pipeline between education and employment is changing shape, not disappearing.

06 Breakout Session 1: Workforce Alignment & Skills Transparency

Small-group table discussion structured around five questions. The following notes are from my table.

Clark — City University of Seattle James Bowen — Aetheon Rose — South Seattle Mary Watts — Boeing Lauren — We Are Washington Employers Mayra DeLanda — South Seattle Bill Wilson — RapidCanvas Lilia Fomai — Seattle Colleges Gabrielle Bachmeier — Highline College Rosella Cruz — South Seattle
What are employers communicating about changing skill requirements?
Clark: Employers are offering free credits and accessible course pathways to help existing employees upskill.
James: Job descriptions have fundamentally shifted. AI fluency used to be listed explicitly as a requirement. Now it is assumed — not mentioned because it is expected.
Lauren: Filter words and inflated job descriptions are creating a trust problem. Some candidates are bypassing online applications entirely and relying on referrals instead.
My Thought
Trust has eroded in online job application systems. Keyword-filter games, inflated requirements, and the prevalence of ghost listings have pushed some qualified candidates away from platforms entirely. This is a structural problem that AI may worsen if it automates both the posting and filtering sides of the pipeline.
What certificate or degree programs have been successful based on workforce needs?
Rosella: Serving marginalized student populations — including women from countries where education was restricted and recent arrivals from Haiti — requires substantial language support. These students need equity-centered entry points. Green River College has workforce certificate programs with a more manual-labor focus. Navigation and tutoring support are critical to retention.
Rosella: English learners should be seen as an asset to workforce programs, not a barrier. The I-BEST (Integrated Basic Education and Skills Training) model, which embeds two instructors — one for language and one for the industry content — is one of the most effective approaches. Highline College and South Seattle College have different workforce program structures; strategic coordination between them is an opportunity.
Lauren: There is a growing tension between higher education's mission to produce lifelong, generalist learners and workforce training's demand for specific technical skills. These roles are beginning to shift in ways that make both harder to deliver.
Mary (Boeing): The most effective programs remove barriers first. Students do not drop out because of academic failure — they drop out because of debt, housing instability, and language barriers. Wrap-around support services, interdisciplinary opportunities, and skills for self-advocacy are just as important as the technical curriculum. Boeing runs a large co-learning program with this philosophy.
Mayra: Critical thinking and technical skills must go together. Colleges are having these AI conversations late — students are already using AI. The question is how to make that usage responsible and intentional.
Lauren: This is not a tool problem. It is a readiness problem.
Mayra: There is a need for an explicit AI literacy course, but there is also significant internal friction at institutions when it comes to adopting AI.
Gabrielle: Colleges are still figuring out policies for both faculty and students. It takes time to develop curriculum, and there is real concern about relevance by the time a course is built and approved.
Lauren: Banks and financial services are significantly ahead of higher education in AI adoption.
How can AI help institutions better understand trends?
Bill: It ultimately comes down to data infrastructure. How data is categorized, where it is pulled from, and what insights it generates are all foundational questions before AI can help.
Mayra: Institutional data is messy. How we gather it, where it comes from, and whether it represents all populations equitably are open questions. The context behind the data matters as much as the data itself.
Bill: Is there bias in the interpretation of data, not just the data itself?
Lauren: At a recent healthcare AI summit, a speaker from Microsoft demonstrated that if you ask an AI for health outcomes for women in Washington State, you cannot know where the underlying data came from. No data integrity. No cultural context in the data. AI in healthcare can be genuinely dangerous when the data infrastructure is not clean or representative.
Clark: Using tools like Perplexity and explainable AI to trace where data comes from can help. We should not take AI outputs at face value — verification against other sources is essential.
Lauren: Instant gratification from AI is a risk. Stanford researchers have flagged that AI systems will confidently give incorrect information. AI is being used as a therapist in some contexts and may be reinforcing unhealthy patterns in users.
What role should employers play in curriculum design in an AI-enabled economy?
James: Build practical applications into the curriculum from the start. Employers want to pipeline talent that can contribute from day one. Right now there is no consistent, repeatable process for doing this well.
James: Deconstruct job descriptions into their component skills, then reconstruct those skills into curriculum or assessment. This is a concrete method for alignment.
Mary: Companies have a responsibility to demystify AI for their education partners.
Gabrielle: Colleges need a unified regional approach to workforce and AI — too many individual institutions are trying to solve this alone.
07 Panel 2: Higher Education
Dr. Kathy Cox — Host
Dr. Rovy Branon — Vice Provost, UW Continuum College
Kristin Copeland — Dean, Clover Park Technical College
Dr. Liz Hollerman — Faculty, Bellevue College
Patrick Rossol-Allison — AVP, Arizona State University
Edith Abbenhaus — Student, PLU / WWU
Edith — on student AI use
AI is part of daily life. She uses it for statistics, Microsoft Excel, note transcription, slide graphics, and timelines. She created a folder, uploaded her textbook PDFs and class slides, and uses AI as an on-demand tutor. It has transformed her relationship with math. Many of her friends use AI to transcribe lecture notes in real time.
Dr. Rovy Branon — on the unresolved AI loop
Nobody has figured this out yet. If faculty are using AI to design courses and AI to grade assignments, and students are using AI to produce those assignments — what exactly is being learned? This creates a genuine integrity problem that sits beneath the surface of most policy conversations.
My Thought — The Dystopian Loop
Rovy named something alarming: we could reach a state where AI generates course content, AI generates student responses, and AI grades those responses. If that loop closes, what is the function of the human in the room? This is not speculative — it is already partially happening. We need to name it clearly before we can design around it.
Patrick Rossol-Allison — on ASU’s AI initiative
ASU partnered with OpenAI and launched a campus-wide challenge inviting faculty, students, and staff to propose what they wanted to build. One result: an AI agent that simulates a patient, allowing medical students to practice diagnosis with no risk to an actual person. This kind of application — reducing risk while preserving the learning experience — is the type of leapfrog solution higher education should be building.
Dr. Rovy Branon — on academic integrity
Cheating is not new. It has existed since ancient China. We must rethink what academic integrity means in this environment, and we must create new ways for students to demonstrate genuine understanding — not just policed versions of old assessments.
Edith — on the gray area
The gray area is enormous. AI is the best tutoring tool she has ever encountered. It answers every question at any hour. The ethical question is not simply "is this cheating?" — it is a larger question about what ethical AI use looks like and who gets to define it.
Kristin Copeland — on the traffic-light system
Faculty have struggled to understand what AI can actually do. Clover Park created a three-tier tagging system for assignments to clarify expectations:
Green — Use AI freely throughout
Yellow — Use AI in a specific, defined way
Red — Do not use AI; it will impede the learning

This system also creates room for a more honest conversation with students. Faculty who are skeptical of AI remain valuable voices in the conversation — their resistance is worth listening to, not overriding.

My Thought — Scaling the Traffic-Light Model
I like this framework a lot. It is honest, flexible, and keeps the learning goal in focus rather than treating AI as inherently good or bad. It could scale beyond individual assignments — what if institutional AI policies used the same structure? Green zones (AI encouraged), yellow zones (AI with disclosure and guardrails), red zones (AI excluded to protect the learning).
Dr. Liz Hollerman — on assessment redesign
Detection tools like Turnitin are not doing what they were designed to do. The better approach is to make the process of producing an assignment as visible as the product. In class, she watches whether critical thinking is actually happening. Assessment has shifted toward projects, portfolios, and collaboration. She asks students to research a nonprofit connected to their own background and community — when students have personal stakes in the material, they become so passionate that the learning is unmistakable.
My Thought — Passion as the Signal
This is the most compelling assessment insight of the day. When a student has genuine stakes in the subject — when it is their community, their background, their question — the learning becomes visible and self-sustaining. Passion is a better signal of authentic engagement than any detection tool.
Patrick Rossol-Allison — on the educator’s shifting role
The educator is more important than ever, but the role is shifting. AI tools are built to maximize engagement — not to challenge assumptions or widen perspectives. That is the irreplaceable function of a human instructor: not to deliver information, but to push thinking in directions the student would not have gone alone.
My Thought — Educators as Coaches
The shift from knowledge-holder to thinking-partner is already happening. The question is whether institutions are intentionally designing for it or just watching it unfold. Coaching requires different skills, different incentives, and different hiring criteria than lecturing.
Edith — on the Socratic method and team projects
Students can use AI as a Socratic partner — asking it questions individually, then bringing those threads back to group brainstorming.
My Thought — Team Projects and Real Stakes
Students often resist team projects. Part of the reason is that the conditions are artificial — no shared culture, no pay, no consequence for disengagement. Real-world teams have shared stakes, company culture, and professional accountability. Designing group work that more closely mirrors those conditions might change student investment.
Kristin Copeland — on change management
This is more a change management challenge than an AI tool challenge. Faculty have redesigned their curricula, courses, and assignments repeatedly over the past two years. What they need is space, time, and the knowledge that their voice is heard. Relationships keep students in school — and relationships keep faculty engaged. We no longer hold exclusive access to knowledge. Students have the same access we do. That changes everything about what the classroom is for. Student voice is as essential as faculty voice.
Dr. Rovy Branon — on identity and loss
We are in a transition period with no clear endpoint. Both blue-collar and white-collar workers face a real loss of professional identity. We ignored what happened to blue-collar workers during earlier waves of automation, and now we are at risk of repeating that pattern with white-collar roles. Higher education cannot afford to be indifferent to its own faculty’s sense of purpose and dignity.
Tension in the Room
Patrick argued that there is no time for grief-based conversations — student success must remain the focus. Kristin argued that we must acknowledge where people are before we can move them forward. Liz agreed with Kristin: removing burden from faculty is a prerequisite, not a luxury. Rovy added: do not forget the staff. This disagreement likely reflects a resource gap — ASU has significantly more institutional capacity than Clover Park Technical College or Bellevue College, and faculty burnout is more acute where resources are thinnest.
Patrick Rossol-Allison — on two categories of AI solutions
There are two distinct buckets: (1) Make everything better with humans — help people learn faster, feel more inspired, reduce friction. (2) Leapfrog solutions — new education models that would not exist without AI. Sense of purpose is more important than AI. AI is a means, not the end.
Audience Q (Lauren) — on intellectual property and trade secrets
Are institutions giving up intellectual property by feeding content into commercial AI systems? Could this eventually cannibalize higher education itself?
Patrick: Faculty research has different rules. What goes into AI systems must be carefully considered. Not all institutional knowledge is safe to share.
Rovy: Higher education may be too big to fail — but AI could enable genuine competitors that look nothing like existing institutions. New kinds of collaboration are also possible. The goal is to empower people, not to protect incumbent structures for their own sake.
Two-thirds of people reportedly believe degrees are no longer worth it. How should higher education respond?
Liz: Change the narrative with evidence. Show students who are succeeding. If we cannot shift that perception, higher education may need to expand its model — certificates, single courses, non-degree pathways — and redefine what success looks like.
Kristin: Reach into K-12 partners. Build relationships earlier. Corporate partnerships with T-Mobile, Microsoft, and others are part of the answer.
Rovy: Continuing education and micro-credentials are already nimble enough to respond. The question is whether the rest of the institution will follow.
Patrick: Higher education is not moving fast enough. Our graduates expect jobs, not just credentials. We need to own that expectation rather than deflecting it.
08 Breakout Session 2: Curriculum Transformation & Future Skills
How is AI currently influencing curriculum decisions?
Mayra: Still unofficial for now. These conversations should have started two years ago. Faculty are implementing individual AI policies without institutional coordination.

James: The skill extraction from job descriptions to curriculum design is still underutilized.
What competencies define an AI-ready graduate across disciplines?
Clark: Confidence. Students who can engage with AI tools, talk about them, explain their choices, and advocate for themselves are AI-ready regardless of their major.
Bill: Adaptability to change. Is college too late to develop this? Should it happen earlier?
Clark: With the current pace of change, everyone — at every age and career stage — has to learn AI now. There is no "too late."
Mayra: Fast assignment turnaround from AI makes it harder to assess genuine competency. We need assessments that surface the thinking behind the output.
Clark: There is a distinction worth naming: AI native vs. AI adopter — analogous to digital native vs. digital immigrant. Native users have grown up with AI as background infrastructure; adopters are learning it as a new skill layer on top of existing practice.

Clark: When we get lost, return to the goal: instill confidence and provide support. Students who feel supported and capable are the output we are building toward.
Kirk: Students are our primary stakeholders.
Shannon: Create psychological safety. Students who are afraid to ask questions are already failing.
Mike: AI can serve as a safe space for students who lack the confidence to ask a human instructor. That is not a replacement for human support — it is a supplement that can lower the barrier to engagement.

Context — Bloom’s Taxonomy
Bloom’s Taxonomy (1956, revised 2001) is a hierarchical model for classifying educational learning objectives across six levels: Remember, Understand, Apply, Analyze, Evaluate, Create. A "Bloom’s Taxonomy of AI" would map AI-related competencies — from recognizing AI output to critically evaluating it to building with it — onto a similar framework.

11 Synthesis

Across all sessions, ten cross-cutting themes emerged. These are patterns that appeared in multiple conversations, not just isolated observations.

Theme 1
Speed Is the Enemy of Equity
The pace of AI adoption systematically favors well-resourced institutions. ASU can partner with OpenAI and run campus-wide challenges. Clover Park and South Seattle are still navigating shared governance. The gap between resource-rich and resource-constrained institutions will widen unless statewide coordination creates shared infrastructure. Lauren’s line said it plainly: speed is the enemy of equity.
Theme 2
Soft Skills Are Still the Universal Ask — But Tech May Be Eroding Them
Every workforce panel speaker named people skills as the top employer priority. Yet much of the technology designed over the past decade — ticketing systems, async-first communication, chatbot interfaces — has removed the human interactions that develop those skills. The workforce is now asking for something that the economy has been quietly de-emphasizing.
Theme 3
Academic Integrity Is Being Redefined, Not Enforced
Detection tools are not working. The conversation has moved from "how do we catch AI use" to "how do we design assessments where AI use is visible, intentional, and doesn’t undermine the learning." Process-based assessment, portfolio models, and passion-anchored assignments are the leading alternatives.
Theme 4
Employer Co-Design Is Essential and Underutilized
Programs designed in the first quarter with real employer input succeed at much higher rates than those designed in isolation. Employers want to participate — they are not passive consumers of graduates. The barrier is usually structural: institutions do not have the processes to integrate employer input early and continuously.
Theme 5
Reciprocal Transparency Is the Trust-Building Mechanism
Meotti’s framing — faculty disclose AI use, students disclose AI use — is not just a policy recommendation. It is the foundation for treating AI as a shared, visible practice rather than a covert arms race between instructors and students. Kristin’s traffic-light system is a practical implementation of this principle at the assignment level.
Theme 6
The Domestication Analogy Changes the Frame
If AI is more like a domesticated animal than a software tool — something with its own behaviors, instincts, and growing autonomy — then the question is not just "how do we use it" but "what kind of relationship are we in with it." That is a different and more demanding question, and higher education has not yet grappled with it seriously.
Theme 7
Data Integrity Is the Unresolved Foundation
Multiple speakers raised the same concern: AI outputs are only as trustworthy as the data they are built on. In education and healthcare, that data is often incomplete, unclean, or culturally non-representative. Insights derived from it will reflect those gaps. Building AI literacy without building data literacy first is building on sand.
Theme 8
AI Literacy Has No Agreed Definition — and That Is a Blocker
Different departments, institutions, and sectors mean different things by "AI literate." A nursing student, a business analyst, and a maritime engineer need different competencies. Without a shared framework — something like a Bloom’s Taxonomy of AI, as Lisa Power proposed — institutions will continue building disconnected and inconsistent curricula.
Theme 9
Faculty Are a Constituency, Not Just a Delivery Channel
Faculty are experiencing a genuine identity crisis as their role shifts from knowledge authority to learning facilitator. Burnout is real, especially at under-resourced institutions. Change management — listening, validating, and involving faculty in designing the transition — is a prerequisite for curriculum change, not a nice-to-have.
Theme 10
The K–12 Gap Is Everyone’s Problem
If students arrive at community colleges and universities without foundational AI fluency, higher education inherits the deficit. K–12, community colleges, universities, and employers are all part of the same pipeline. A statewide coalition — as proposed by K. Skinner-Cox — is the only structural response adequate to the scale of the challenge.
12 Questions Raised

The following questions emerged across all sessions. They are organized into two groups: questions posed during structured discussions (asked of the room, often left partially answered) and open questions the summit surfaced (larger, systemic questions with no clear answer yet).

Workforce & Employer Alignment
What are employers actually communicating about changing skill requirements — and are those signals clear enough for institutions to act on?
Raised in Session 1. James noted that AI fluency is now assumed in job descriptions rather than listed. Lauren flagged that keyword filters and inflated requirements have eroded trust in the online application process. The consensus: demand signals from employers exist but are noisy, inconsistent, and sector-specific.
What role should employers play in curriculum design in an AI-enabled economy — and who is responsible for making that collaboration happen?
Raised in Session 1 and Panel 1. Lauren: programs co-designed with employers in the first quarter succeed. James: there is no consistent, repeatable process for doing this. Gabrielle: colleges need a unified regional approach rather than every institution solving it alone. No concrete owner was identified.
How quickly should curricula be updated to match the pace of AI adoption in industry — and who decides when they are out of date?
Raised by Emily in Panel 1. Microsoft reports 30% of its code is written by AI. Ingrid: the answer is sector-specific — tech differs from maritime, which differs from healthcare. No institution-level answer was given.
How do smaller, under-resourced institutions keep pace with the AI transition without falling further behind larger, well-funded universities?
Raised implicitly throughout. Lauren named it directly: speed is the enemy of equity. Multi-institutional programs were proposed as a structural response, but no specific model was described for Washington State.
Curriculum & Program Design
What competencies define an AI-ready graduate — and should that definition vary by discipline, or is there a common core?
Raised in Session 2. Clark proposed confidence and adaptability as cross-disciplinary anchors. The table did not reach a shared definition. Lisa Power’s closing proposal — a Bloom’s Taxonomy of AI — is the closest thing to a framework that was surfaced.
How should AI literacy be integrated across all curricula rather than siloed into a single course?
Raised in Session 2. Mayra noted the absence of coordination across departments. Clark described City University’s approach: "AI infusion" (embedding AI across existing programs) plus standalone AI degree pathways. No consensus model emerged for community colleges.
Is higher education structurally capable of being responsive enough to fast-changing workforce needs — or does that role belong to continuing education and micro-credentials?
Raised in Panel 2. Rovy: continuing education is ready to move fast. Patrick: higher education as a whole is not fast enough. Kristin: bureaucracy and shared governance are the main structural blockers. No resolution.
Academic Integrity & Assessment
What does academic integrity actually mean in an AI-enabled world — and who gets to redefine it?
Central question in Panel 2 and Session 3. Rovy: we need to rethink integrity entirely, not just enforce old rules. Edith: the gray area is enormous. Liz: shift the focus from the product to the process. No institutional consensus definition was offered.
How do we distinguish genuine learning from AI-assisted production when AI detection tools are unreliable?
Raised in Session 3. Shannon: detection is not doing what it is supposed to do. Clark: Turnitin can be bypassed. Liz’s approach — make the process visible, watch for critical thinking in class — was the most concrete answer, but it does not scale easily to large or fully online courses.
If grades are no longer a reliable signal of learning, what should replace them?
Raised in Session 3. Kirk: some institutions have moved to portfolio and documented-learning models. Liz: projects, process documentation, and collaboration. No standard alternative was agreed on.
AI Literacy & Equity
Who owns AI literacy in an institution — and what does "AI literate" even mean?
Raised directly in Session 3. Shannon: the scope is undefined and constantly shifting. Clark: anchor it to dispositions and behaviors, not tools. Mary: the right scope depends on the academic program. No institution claimed ownership; no shared definition was agreed on.
How do we support students who are already behind in digital literacy before we layer AI on top?
Raised across Session 1 and Session 3. Rosella: some students are not literate in their own language. Handa: some younger students have no desktop experience at all. Libby: some of the people Goodwill serves have never used a computer mouse. No scalable solution was proposed.
How do students whose first language is not English learn to prompt AI effectively — and whose job is it to teach them?
Raised in Panel 1 (Libby) and Session 1 (Rosella). The I-BEST model of co-instruction was offered as a partial answer for foundational skills, but was not connected to AI prompting specifically. This gap remained unaddressed.
Big Picture & Still Open
If the technology optimists are right about where AI is going, what does higher education need to look like to be ready?
Posed by Meotti to close the keynote. Not answered. Intentionally left open as the framing challenge for the entire summit.
Are institutions giving up intellectual property to commercial AI systems in ways that could ultimately cannibalize higher education itself?
Raised by Lauren during Panel 2. Patrick: faculty research has different rules and must be handled carefully. Rovy: AI could enable genuine competitors to higher education. No policy guidance was offered.
How can higher education be proactive in shaping AI policy alongside corporations and government, rather than always reacting to decisions made elsewhere?
Raised by Liza Burke in the closing discussion. Framed as both a political and structural challenge. A Washington State coalition was proposed by K. Skinner-Cox as a starting point. No mechanism was defined.
Are these conversations happening at the K–12 level — and if not, who is responsible for closing that gap?
Raised by Kirk in the closing discussion and echoed by a participant referencing a K–12 AI initiative involving Trinh Pham. The statewide coalition concept was the proposed answer, but nothing was formalized.
What is the irreducible human contribution in the classroom — and what would we insist on keeping human even if AI could do it?
Not asked directly during the summit, but surfaced by the AI-talks-to-AI concern Rovy raised. This may be the most important question the summit circled without landing on.
13 My Own Thoughts

The following are personal observations, questions, and ideas that emerged throughout the day. These are not summaries of what others said — they are my own reactions, some of which push back against or complicate what was presented.

Thought 01
The Dual-AI Cultural Simulation
James Bowen and I talked about implicit bias in AI assessment. This sparked an idea: what if we trained one AI model on one cultural context — its language, history, social norms, legal frameworks, values — and a second AI on a different context entirely? Then we placed both into the same scenario — say, evaluating a student essay, assessing a job candidate, or resolving a community conflict — and compared their outputs.
The point would not be to declare one model correct. It would be to make abstract AI bias concrete and visible. Students could observe how the same situation produces different judgments depending on what the model was trained to expect. This could be one of the most powerful AI literacy exercises possible — grounded, observable, and genuinely surprising.
Thought 02
The Soft Skills Paradox
Every workforce speaker said some version of: "Employers want people skills more than anything else." But the technology industry — the same industry now driving the AI agenda in education — spent the last decade designing products specifically to reduce human interaction. Tickets instead of conversations. Pings instead of meetings. Automated responses instead of phone calls.
We often hear that soft skills are the most critical asset in the modern workforce. However, much of the technology we build and use is designed to remove human friction — replacing conversations with tickets and meetings with pings. Is the tech industry inadvertently atrophying the very people skills it claims to value most?
Thought 03
AI in Conflict and Negative Relationships
Lauren said that during a real student crisis, they chose human relationship skills over AI. That makes sense. But it raised a different question for me: what about situations where human emotion produces harmful outcomes?
In a crisis, we chose humans over AI because we needed empathy and relationship. But in a geopolitical conflict or a corporate dispute — a situation where human emotion drives escalation — is AI’s emotional neutrality actually an asset? Or does removing guilt and empathy from the decision loop just make it easier to stay in conflict without feeling the cost?
Thought 04
AI-Assisted Prompting for ESL Learners
Libby noted that workers who are not native English speakers, or who are unfamiliar with U.S. job market norms, often struggle to prompt AI effectively and therefore get poor results. This is a compounding disadvantage: the people who most need AI as a support tool are the least equipped to use it well.
There may be a design opportunity here: AI tools that scaffold the prompting process for ESL learners — helping them articulate what they want before they ask, rather than after they get a bad answer. Claude’s existing language capabilities are strong. The question is whether anyone is designing for this user explicitly.
Thought 05
Traffic-Light Tags for Institutional AI Policy
Kristin’s green/yellow/red assignment tagging system is the most immediately actionable idea from the day. It is honest, flexible, and keeps the learning goal in focus rather than treating AI as categorically good or bad.
The same framework could scale to institutional AI policy: green zones where AI is encouraged (administrative efficiency, accessibility tools, research synthesis), yellow zones where AI is permitted with disclosure and guardrails (student assessments, faculty course design), and red zones where AI is excluded because the learning or trust at stake requires full human engagement (clinical placements, crisis response, mentorship conversations).
Thought 06
Passion as the Most Reliable Assessment Signal
Liz’s example of students researching nonprofits tied to their own backgrounds — and becoming so engaged that the learning was unmistakable — is the most compelling assessment redesign idea of the entire day. No detection tool needed. No rubric required. Passion is self-evidencing.
If genuine stakes produce genuine engagement, the design question is: how do we create more assignments where students have real skin in the game? Not artificial stakes (grades), but actual relevance to something they care about. This probably means treating student identity and community as curriculum, not just context.
Thought 07
Educators as Coaches — Designed or Accidental?
Patrick and Liz both pointed toward the educator becoming more of a coach and less of a content deliverer. The shift is real. But the question is whether institutions are intentionally redesigning for it, or simply watching it happen and hoping for the best.
Coaching requires different skills than lecturing — active listening, adaptive questioning, emotional attunement. It requires different incentives and different hiring criteria. If we want educators to be coaches, we need to hire, train, evaluate, and reward them as coaches. Right now most institutions still hire and evaluate for content expertise.
Thought 08
Why Students Resist Team Projects
Edith mentioned that students use AI for the Socratic method individually before bringing ideas to a group. This is interesting, but it also raised a question about why team projects are often received with reluctance.
Real-world teams work because they have shared stakes, organizational culture, professional consequences, and compensation. School team projects have none of those things. There is no culture alignment, no pay, no meaningful consequence for a disengaged teammate. If we want team projects to feel real, we need to get closer to those conditions — or be honest that what we are teaching is a simulation, not the real thing.
Thought 09
The AI-Talks-to-AI Loop
Rovy raised this and it has stayed with me. If faculty use AI to design courses and AI to grade work, and students use AI to write assignments — the human is increasingly a relay station, not a participant.
We should name this clearly and ask: what is the irreducible human contribution in the classroom? Not what AI cannot do yet — but what we would insist on keeping human even if AI could do it. That question might be the most important one higher education needs to answer, and it was not asked directly today.
Thought 10
Trust Has Eroded in Online Job Applications
Lauren mentioned that some candidates skip job boards entirely and rely on referrals. James noted that AI fluency is now assumed rather than listed in job descriptions.
Both observations point to the same underlying problem: the formal systems — job listings, applications, keyword filters — have become so gamed that people with real options are routing around them. This is a signal about institutional trust, not just job market mechanics. If education credentials are next to face this kind of trust erosion, that is a serious risk to address proactively.
Thought 11
Multi-AI Comparison as an AI Literacy Exercise
I raised this briefly in Session 3: running the same question through multiple AI models with different training data, alignment approaches, or system prompts, then comparing and discussing the outputs.
This could become a standard classroom exercise in AI literacy — one that requires no special tools, just access to two or more models. Students who learn to notice when Claude says X, ChatGPT says Y, and Perplexity says Z — and who can interrogate why — are developing genuine critical AI thinking rather than just AI fluency. The difference matters.
