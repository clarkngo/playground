Step 5 — Mask email and SSN
from pyspark.sql.functions import regexp_replace # Mask email: keep first character and domain, hide middle # alice@health.com => a***@health.com df_masked = df.withColumn( "email", regexp_replace("email", r"(^.).*(@.*$)", r"\1***\2") ) # Mask SSN: hide area and group numbers, keep serial (last 4) # 123-45-6789 => ***-**-6789 df_masked = df_masked.withColumn( "ssn", regexp_replace("ssn", r"\d{3}-\d{2}", "***-**") )
Step 6 — Display and verify masked output
# Step 6: Confirm masking was applied correctly display(df_masked) # Also verify no raw PII leaks remain in the masked columns df_masked.select("patient_id", "email", "ssn").show(truncate=False)
Step 7 — Save as secure table for analysts
# Step 7: Persist masked dataset as a separate, analyst-safe table df_masked.write.mode("overwrite").saveAsTable("patients_secure") # Result: # patients => Admin access only (raw PII) # patients_secure => Analyst access (masked PII)

ℹ  Two tables now exist: patients (raw, admin-only) and patients_secure (masked, analyst-safe). This separation is the technical foundation of the RBAC policy defined in Part 4.

📚 Why this answer? ►

Why use regex masking instead of simply deleting the column?

Deleting the email column entirely would break any analytics that need to count unique users or detect duplicate accounts. Masking preserves the structure and referential value (you can still tell two rows have different emails) while eliminating the personally identifiable content. This is the principle of data minimization in practice: keep only what the business function requires, nothing more.

Why retain the last 4 digits of the SSN?

The last 4 digits of a US SSN are the serial number and are the most commonly used for identity verification in customer service contexts (“last four of your social”). Retaining them allows the organization to perform soft identity checks without exposing the full SSN. The first 5 digits (area number + group number) are what enable full identity theft, so masking those is the critical control. This is a deliberate design tradeoff, not an oversight.

Why save as a separate table instead of modifying the original?

The original patients table is the system of record. If you overwrite it with masked data, you lose the ability to perform legitimate administrative operations that require the full data (e.g., auditing, responding to a HIPAA data subject request). Separation also makes the access control model explicit and auditable: whoever can query patients has been explicitly granted elevated access, and whoever queries patients_secure has not. This is a cleaner, more defensible architecture than a single table with row-level security filters.

📚 Why this answer? ►

Why tag at the column level, not just the table level?

Tagging only the table as “sensitive” tells you nothing about which specific fields carry risk. If the country column is public but ssn is PII, a table-level restriction would either over-block (analysts cannot see country) or under-block (everyone sees SSN). Column-level tagging allows precise, field-by-field access control and is the foundation of any compliant data governance catalog under HIPAA and GDPR.

Why is diagnosis classified as Confidential rather than PII?

PII refers to data that identifies a specific person — name, email, SSN. diagnosis is not itself an identifier, but it is Protected Health Information (PHI) under HIPAA because it describes a person’s health status. Using the “Confidential” tag separates the identity risk (PII) from the privacy risk (health disclosure). This distinction matters operationally: the mitigation for PII is masking; the mitigation for PHI is access restriction entirely.

Why is name tagged PII but left visible in the analyst view?

This is a deliberate design choice in this scenario: analysts need to identify records by name for audit queries. In a production HIPAA environment, name would also be masked or replaced with a pseudonymous ID. This case study uses a simplified model to keep the masking exercise focused on the highest-risk fields (email and SSN). In a real implementation, all PII columns should be subject to masking or access restriction — not just the subset demonstrated here.

📚 Why this answer? ►

Why is the access matrix the correct deliverable here, not just a roles list?

A roles list tells you what roles exist. An access matrix tells you what each role can actually see for each field. This is the difference between a policy and a testable control. When an auditor asks “can an analyst see patient SSNs?” the matrix gives a direct, verifiable answer. It also exposes gaps — in the pre-breach state, the contractor row would have shown “Full access” for SSN, making the risk immediately visible.

Why do contractors get no direct table access?

Contractors represent the highest-risk access category in this breach scenario. They are external to the organization, may not be subject to the same security training, and their access is often time-limited with unclear offboarding procedures. The principle of least privilege means their access should be the minimum necessary for their business function. If a contractor needs to analyze patient trends, they receive a pre-aggregated report — not a query connection to the database.

Why does the analyst still see country in full?

country is a geographic aggregate, not an individual identifier. Knowing that 3 out of 4 patients are from the USA does not identify any individual. Restricting it would prevent analysts from doing legitimate geographic analysis (e.g., disease prevalence by country) with zero security benefit. Good data governance is not about maximum restriction — it is about appropriate restriction. Over-restricting data makes governance a liability (analysts find workarounds) rather than an asset.

Part 4 Security Policies Deliverable: 3–5 Governance Policies +
Answer — Step 14 — HealthSecure Inc. Data Security Policies v1.0
Policy 1 — Data Masking
  • All PII fields (email, ssn) must be masked before any non-admin user can query or export them.
  • Masking is applied at the table level via the patients_secure view — not as a per-query filter, which could be bypassed.
  • Masked values must preserve enough structure for legitimate use (e.g., last 4 digits of SSN, domain of email) without exposing re-identifiable content.
  • Any new PII column added to the patients dataset must be masked within 5 business days of creation and before any analyst access is granted.
  • Masking logic must be documented in the data catalog with the masking pattern and date of implementation.
Policy 2 — Access Control (RBAC)
  • Access to patient data is governed by role assignment in OpenMetadata and enforced at the Databricks table level.
  • The Admin role is reserved for internal staff with a documented business need for raw patient data; all assignments require written approval from the Data Owner.
  • External contractors and third-party systems must not be granted direct query access to any table containing PII or PHI; access is limited to pre-approved aggregated datasets.
  • All role assignments are reviewed quarterly; inactive users are revoked automatically after 60 days without a login event.
  • Shared credentials (one login used by multiple people) are strictly prohibited; every user must have an individual, auditable identity.
Policy 3 — Data Classification
  • All columns in every patient-related table must carry a DataSensitivity tag (PII, Confidential, or Public) before the table may be shared with any internal or external team.
  • Tagging is performed by the Data Steward and reviewed by the Data Owner within 10 business days of a new table or column being added.
  • Untagged columns are treated as Confidential by default until classified; this conservative default prevents accidental PII exposure during onboarding of new datasets.
  • Classification must be revisited whenever a column’s data changes (e.g., a column that previously held country codes now holds city-level GPS coordinates upgrades from Public to PII).
Policy 4 — Audit Logging
  • All queries against tables containing PII or PHI columns must be logged with: timestamp, user identity, role, query text, and row count returned.
  • Audit logs are immutable and retained for a minimum of 7 years in compliance with HIPAA record-keeping requirements.
  • Access to audit logs is restricted to the Admin role and Compliance team only.
  • Automated alerts are triggered for: queries outside business hours, bulk exports exceeding 500 rows, and any access attempt by a user with a revoked role.
Policy 5 — Breach Notification
  • Any confirmed or suspected exposure of PII or PHI must be reported to the Data Owner and Legal team within 24 hours of detection.
  • HIPAA requires formal notification to affected patients within 60 days of discovering a breach; this timeline begins from the date of detection, not the date the breach occurred.
  • A post-incident report documenting root cause, scope, remediation steps, and policy updates must be completed within 30 days of breach resolution.
  • All staff with data access must complete annual HIPAA security training; failure to complete training results in immediate access suspension until the requirement is fulfilled.
📚 Why this answer? ►

Why are policies necessary if the technical controls already exist?

Technical controls enforce governance in the system. Policies enforce it in the organization. Masking in Databricks prevents an analyst from accidentally seeing an SSN today, but it does not prevent a future engineer from removing the masking when they add a new column. A written policy means that decision has a governance checkpoint, requires approval, and creates an audit trail. Without policy, every technical control is one database migration away from being removed.

Why default to Confidential for untagged columns?

The cost asymmetry strongly favors a conservative default. If an untagged column is actually Public and you treat it as Confidential, the only cost is that analysts must submit a classification request before using it — a minor inconvenience. If an untagged column is actually PII and you treat it as Public, you have a potential HIPAA violation every time an analyst queries it. Defaulting to the more restrictive classification forces a deliberate classification decision rather than assuming safety.

Why 60 days for HIPAA breach notification?

This is not an internal best practice — it is a legal requirement under the HIPAA Breach Notification Rule (45 CFR Part 164, Subpart D). Failure to notify within 60 days exposes the organization to fines up to $1.9 million per violation category per year. Policy 5 exists to ensure the legal timeline is known and operationalized before a breach occurs, not discovered during the chaos of incident response.

📚 Why this answer? ►

Why include Likelihood as a separate column from Impact?

Impact alone tells you how bad a breach would be if it happened. Likelihood tells you how soon you should expect it to happen. A risk with High Impact and High Likelihood (like unmasked PII in an analyst-accessible table) must be addressed immediately. A risk with High Impact but Low Likelihood (like no breach notification process) can be addressed on a planned timeline. Without both dimensions, all high-impact risks look equally urgent and the organization has no basis for sequencing remediation work.

Why add “Stale access” as a risk when no breach has occurred from it yet?

The original breach was caused by over-permissioned users. Stale access is the same vulnerability deferred in time — a user who left three months ago but still has an active admin account is an over-permissioned user waiting to become a breach. Risk registers must anticipate systemic vulnerabilities, not just document what has already gone wrong. The “no breach has occurred yet” framing is a survivorship bias trap: you only know it has not been exploited; you do not know if someone has already accessed the account.

Why is “schema change introduces new PII column” a risk worth including?

This is a governance continuity risk. Most data breaches involving new PII fields do not happen because someone maliciously added them — they happen because an engineer added a new column for a legitimate feature (e.g., phone numbers for 2FA) and nobody updated the masking, classification, or access control rules to account for it. Without a process that treats schema changes as governance events, every new feature is a potential regression in your security posture. The risk register forces the organization to design a schema change review process before the gap is exploited.

Reflection
Why does healthcare data require stricter governance than retail data? What is HIPAA and what happens without it?

Retail data governs what you bought and where you live. Healthcare data governs your body — your diagnoses, medications, mental health history, and genetic conditions. The harm from healthcare data exposure is irreversible: a patient who has their SSN and diagnosis leaked cannot un-expose their HIV status or their psychiatric history to future employers, insurers, or the public. The potential for discrimination, stigma, and financial harm is categorically more severe than a credit card breach where the card can simply be cancelled.

HIPAA (Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, 1996) is the US federal law that governs how healthcare organizations must protect Protected Health Information (PHI) — any information that can identify a patient and relates to their health condition, care, or payment. HIPAA requires access controls, audit logging, encryption, workforce training, and breach notification within 60 days. Violations carry civil penalties up to $1.9 million per violation category per year, and willful neglect with no correction can result in criminal prosecution.

Without governance, HealthSecure Inc. is not just operationally at risk — it is legally exposed. Every day the patients table was accessible to over-permissioned analysts was a day of ongoing HIPAA violation. Data governance in healthcare is not optional best practice; it is the legal floor. The remediation in this case study is the minimum required to return to compliance, not an aspirational target.
