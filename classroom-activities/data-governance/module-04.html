Data Lifecycle Management

🏭 Case Study: “FinBank — The Compliance Audit Failure”

FinBank, a financial services company, failed a regulatory compliance audit because historical customer records were missing, expired data was never deleted, and no retention proof existed. Students act as the Data Governance Sustainment Team to design and implement a full lifecycle management system: active/archive partitioning, deletion workflow, Delta version tracking, automation scripts, and OpenMetadata lifecycle tags.

Part 1 Dataset Setup & Active / Historical Partitioning Steps 1–5 · D1 +
✓ Complete Answer — Parts 1–5

Steps 1–2 are environment setup (upload CSV, create notebook). Step 3 loads the dataset. Steps 4–5 apply the core lifecycle split: active data is transactions from the last 12 months; everything older is historical.

Step 3 — Load Dataset
df = spark.read.csv( "/FileStore/tables/Transactions.csv", header=True, inferSchema=True ) display(df)
Step 4 — Create Active Dataset (≤365 days old)
from pyspark.sql.functions import col, current_date, datediff active_data = df.filter( datediff(current_date(), col("transaction_date")) <= 365 ) display(active_data)
Step 5 — Create Historical Dataset (>365 days old)
historical_data = df.filter( datediff(current_date(), col("transaction_date")) > 365 ) display(historical_data)

💡 Why This Answer? ▶

Why 365 days as the active/archive boundary?

The 12-month rule is the most common financial services lifecycle threshold. Transactions within the last year are needed for operational queries, customer service lookups, fraud detection, and regulatory reporting. Beyond 12 months, transactions are still legally required but are rarely queried operationally — making them ideal candidates for archive storage, which is cheaper and has lower IOPS requirements.

Why use datediff(current_date(), col(...)) rather than a hardcoded date?

Dynamic filtering means the query produces the correct result on any day it runs. A hardcoded date like WHERE transaction_date < '2025-04-28' becomes stale immediately. Using current_date() ensures the lifecycle boundary automatically advances with each execution — critical for scheduled automation scripts that run daily or weekly.

Why inferSchema=True?

Without inferSchema, all columns default to StringType. The transaction_date column must be parsed as DateType for datediff() to work correctly. If the date is a string, Spark will throw a type mismatch error. Always verify the schema with df.printSchema() after loading.

Part 2 Archive Process — Save & Verify Tables Steps 6–8 · D1 +
✓ Complete Answer — Steps 6–8
Step 6 — Save Active Table
active_data.write.mode("overwrite").saveAsTable( "transactions_active" )
Step 7 — Save Archive Table
historical_data.write.mode("overwrite").saveAsTable( "transactions_archive" )
Step 8 — Verify Archive Movement (SQL)
SELECT COUNT(*) AS active_count -- Should be ~60-80 FROM transactions_active; SELECT COUNT(*) AS archive_count -- Should be ~420-440 FROM transactions_archive; -- Sanity check: totals must equal 500 SELECT (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM transactions_active) + (SELECT COUNT(*) FROM transactions_archive) AS total_rows;
💡 Why This Answer? ▶

Why mode("overwrite")?

The lifecycle pipeline is designed to be re-run on a schedule (daily, weekly). overwrite ensures the table reflects the most current partition — as new transactions become historical with time, they automatically migrate from active to archive on the next run. Using append would cause duplicates on every execution.

Why verify with COUNT(*) after saving?

The verification query is the compliance proof. Auditors need evidence that the archive was created and that all rows were accounted for. The sanity check (active + archive = 500) confirms no rows were lost or duplicated during the partitioning. This count becomes part of the audit trail alongside the timestamp of execution.

Two separate tables vs. one table with a lifecycle column?

Two tables is the correct enterprise pattern for lifecycle management. A single table with a lifecycle_stage column still stores everything together — it does not reduce storage costs or query overhead. Separate tables allow: (1) different storage tiers in cloud providers, (2) different access policies via RBAC, (3) different retention rules per table, and (4) simpler compliance audits.

Part 3 Deletion Workflow — 7-Year Retention Rule Steps 9–11 · D3 +
✓ Complete Answer — Steps 9–11
Retention Policy — Step 9: Delete data older than 7 years (2,555 days). Financial services regulations (including Dodd-Frank, SOX, and various banking acts) require transaction records to be retained for a minimum of 7 years from the date of the transaction. After this period, data must be deleted to comply with data minimization principles under GDPR and equivalent regulations.
Step 10 — Identify Expired Data (>7 years = 2,555 days)
expired_data = df.filter( datediff(current_date(), col("transaction_date")) > 2555 ) display(expired_data) # As of 2026-04-28: transactions before ~2019-05-11 are expired # This captures all 2017 and 2018 records in the dataset
Step 11 — Simulate Delete (retain everything else)
# Retained = all data that is NOT expired retained_data = df.filter( datediff(current_date(), col("transaction_date")) <= 2555 ) display(retained_data) # Save retained data back (simulated deletion) retained_data.write.mode("overwrite").saveAsTable( "transactions_retained" ) # Log the deletion event expired_count = expired_data.count() print(f"Expired rows identified for deletion: {expired_count}") print(f"Retained rows: {retained_data.count()}") print(f"Deletion policy: 7 years (2555 days)")
Expected Output (as of 2026-04-28)
CategoryDate RangeApprox. RowsAction
ExpiredBefore 2019-05-11 (2017–early 2019)~60–80 rowsMark for deletion
Retained2019-05-11 onwards~420–440 rowsKeep in transactions_retained
💡 Why This Answer? ▶

Why 2,555 days (not 7 × 365 = 2,555)?

2,555 = 7 × 365. This does not account for leap years — a more precise value would be 2,557 days (7 years with 2 leap years). In production, the recommended approach is to use date arithmetic directly: transaction_date < date_sub(current_date(), 365 * 7) or equivalent month-based functions. For this lab, 2,555 is the accepted approximation.

Why is this a simulation and not an actual DELETE?

In Databricks, the safest deletion pattern for compliance purposes is: (1) identify the expired rows, (2) save the retained subset to a new table, (3) log the count and timestamp, and (4) only then drop or overwrite the original. This two-step approach ensures there is always a recoverable state. Direct DELETE FROM statements on large tables also hold locks and can fail partway through without a transactional rollback.

Why must you log the deletion event?

Regulators do not just require that data is deleted — they require proof of deletion. The log output (count of expired rows, timestamp, policy name) forms the deletion record. Without it, FinBank would face the same audit failure in the next review cycle. In production, this log should be written to an immutable audit store such as an S3 bucket with Object Lock or Azure Blob Storage with Immutability Policies.

Part 4 Delta Version Tracking — History & Rollback Steps 12–14 · D1 +
✓ Complete Answer — Steps 12–14
Step 12 — Save as Delta Table
active_data.write.format("delta")\ .mode("overwrite")\ .saveAsTable("transactions_delta")
Step 13 — Update Status for Low-Value Transactions (SQL)
UPDATE transactions_delta SET status = 'Archived' WHERE amount < 100; -- Verify the update SELECT transaction_id, amount, status FROM transactions_delta WHERE status = 'Archived' ORDER BY amount ASC;
Step 14 — View Delta History
DESCRIBE HISTORY transactions_delta;
DESCRIBE HISTORY Output — Example

💡 Why This Answer? ▶

Why Delta format instead of Parquet or CSV?

Delta Lake adds ACID transactions and versioned history on top of Parquet storage. Standard Parquet files do not support UPDATE or DELETE operations natively — you would have to rewrite the entire file. Delta allows row-level modifications with full transaction isolation. More importantly, DESCRIBE HISTORY creates the audit trail that FinBank needs to demonstrate to regulators that every data change was logged with a timestamp and operation type.

Why update status to "Archived" for amount < 100?

Low-value micro-transactions (below $100) are frequently the first candidates for lifecycle stage changes in financial services — they are rarely disputed, rarely audited, and their operational value decays quickly. This step demonstrates that Delta updates are selective: you can change a column value for a subset of rows while leaving the rest untouched, and the history captures exactly which rows were affected and when.

What is the business value of DESCRIBE HISTORY?

Version history answers the auditor question: “When was this data modified, by whom, and what changed?” Without it, FinBank cannot demonstrate data integrity. With it, they can: (1) roll back accidental updates with RESTORE TABLE, (2) run time-travel queries with SELECT * FROM transactions_delta VERSION AS OF 0, and (3) provide the regulator with a tamper-evident change log for any table in the data platform.

Part 5 Lifecycle Automation Script & Audit Logging Steps 15–16 · D1 +
✓ Complete Answer — Steps 15–16
Step 15 — Archive Automation Script
import shutil import os from datetime import datetime source = "/dbfs/FileStore/tables/Transactions.csv" archive = "/dbfs/FileStore/archive/Transactions.csv" # Ensure the archive directory exists os.makedirs("/dbfs/FileStore/archive/", exist_ok=True) # Copy source file to archive location shutil.copy(source, archive) print("Archive completed:", datetime.now())
Step 16 — Structured Audit Log
log = { "action" : "archive", "file" : "Transactions.csv", "source" : "/dbfs/FileStore/tables/Transactions.csv", "destination": "/dbfs/FileStore/archive/Transactions.csv", "timestamp" : str(datetime.now()), "policy" : "7-year retention", "executed_by": "Data Governance Sustainment Team" } print(log)

{'action': 'archive', 'file': 'Transactions.csv', 'timestamp': '2026-04-28 10:15:32.441023', 'policy': '7-year retention', 'executed_by': 'Data Governance Sustainment Team'}
💡 Why This Answer? ▶

Why use shutil.copy instead of moving the file?

Copy, then verify, then delete is the safest lifecycle automation pattern. shutil.move() removes the source immediately — if the copy fails midway, the file is gone. Copying first preserves the source until you have verified the archive is intact. Only after the copy succeeds (and the count checks pass) should the original be removed in a follow-up step.

Why include policy and executed_by in the log?

The structured log dictionary is not just debugging output — it is governance metadata. In production, this dictionary would be written to a permanent audit store (Delta table, S3 JSON, or a compliance database). The policy field links the action to the specific retention rule that authorized the deletion. The executed_by field identifies accountability. Auditors reviewing the log can trace exactly: what was archived, when, by whom, and under which policy authority.

How would you schedule this in production?

Databricks Jobs (formerly Databricks Workflows) can schedule this notebook to run automatically on a cron schedule — e.g., daily at 2:00 AM. The scheduler handles retries on failure, sends alerts on success/failure, and logs each run. Combined with Delta Lake history, this creates a fully automated, auditable lifecycle pipeline with no manual intervention required.

💡 Why This Answer? ▶

Why does OpenMetadata tagging matter if the data already lives in separate tables?

The Databricks tables enforce the physical separation of data. OpenMetadata tags provide governance visibility — they make the lifecycle stage discoverable to any data consumer who finds the table in the catalog without reading the documentation. A data analyst querying transactions_archive who sees the Archived tag immediately knows: this is read-only, it is not the live dataset, and they should not use it for real-time reporting. Tags reduce miscommunication between data engineers and business consumers.

Why does the recovery strategy specify RTO (Recovery Time Objective)?

An RTO without a number is not a recovery strategy — it is a wishlist. Regulators and internal SLAs require specific time commitments. The RTO values above (<1 hour for accidental deletion, <2 hours for system failure) are derived from the simplicity of Delta time-travel recovery. These should be tested in a DR drill before they are committed to in a policy document. If the actual recovery takes 4 hours, the policy must be updated.

Why is “lineage + history” the answer for compliance audits?

A compliance audit is fundamentally a provenance question: where did this data come from, what happened to it, and can you prove it? Delta Lake answers the “what happened” question (every operation logged with timestamp). OpenMetadata lineage answers the “where did it come from” question (CSV → raw table → active/archive tables). Together they create the complete chain of custody that regulators require. Neither tool alone is sufficient.

Reflection
Why is data lifecycle management a compliance requirement and not just a storage optimization?

Storage optimization is a side effect. Compliance is the primary driver. Financial regulations — SOX, Dodd-Frank, PCI DSS, GDPR, and country-level banking acts — specify exactly how long transaction records must be retained and require proof that expired records were deleted. FinBank failed its audit not because it was storing too much data, but because it could not prove what it had, where it was, and what happened to it. That is a governance failure, not a storage failure.

Data without a lifecycle policy degrades in three ways simultaneously: it accumulates (storage costs rise), it becomes unreliable (active and stale data are indistinguishable), and it creates legal exposure (inability to prove deletion under GDPR right-to-erasure requests). Each of these is a separate risk category with separate penalties. The lifecycle pipeline built in this module addresses all three: separate tables enforce reliability, the deletion workflow controls accumulation, and the audit log proves compliance.

The deeper lesson is that data governance is the enforcement layer between policy (the 7-year retention rule exists in law) and practice (nobody implemented it in the system). Without Databricks lifecycle filters, OpenMetadata tags, and Delta audit history, the policy exists only on paper. A governance framework that lives only in a document has no operational effect — and provides no protection when the auditor walks in.
