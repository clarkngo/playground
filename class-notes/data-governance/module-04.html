Data Sustainment

Keeping governance alive — planning, controls, quality, and long-term stewardship
Data Sustainment Roles & Controls Quality & Validation Security & Lifecycle
01 What is Data Sustainment?
Definition
Data Sustainment
The ongoing discipline of maintaining data governance over time — ensuring that data remains accurate, accessible, compliant, and useful as the organization, its systems, and its requirements evolve. Governance is not a project you finish; it is an operating model you continuously run.

Building governance is the easy part. Sustaining it is where most organizations struggle. Systems change, teams grow, regulations update, and old data accumulates. Without active sustainment, even the best initial governance framework decays within 12–18 months.

Data sustainment rests on three pillars:

🗺️
Planning
Governance roadmaps, data SLAs, capacity plans, and scheduled reviews. You define what "good" looks like and how you will get there.
📋
Policies
Formal rules that define what is required — data retention schedules, access control policies, classification standards, quality thresholds.
⚙️
Best Practices
Repeatable, proven methods for maintaining data quality, onboarding new systems, handling incidents, and evolving the governance framework.
Analogy
Think of a city’s water infrastructure. Building the pipes and treatment plants is governance setup. Sustainment is the ongoing work — pressure monitoring, pipe inspections, water quality testing, and emergency repairs — that keeps the water safe to drink every day. No city builds infrastructure and then walks away.
02 Governance Roles & Hats

Different people in an organization wear different “hats” in data governance. Each hat has a category of concern and a set of key tasks. Confusing these roles leads to gaps (nobody owns quality) or overlaps (two teams fighting over the same data).

Role Matrix

Key Insight
In small organizations, one person often wears multiple hats — for example, a data engineer may be both Custodian and Producer. The key is that every hat is assigned to someone. Unassigned roles are the most common source of data governance failures.
03 People Layers — Strategic to Operational

Data governance people are organized into three layers. Each layer has a different time horizon (years vs. weeks vs. daily), a different focus (direction vs. process vs. execution), and different accountabilities.

S
Strategic Layer — The Board & Senior Management
Sets the what and why. Defines which data domains matter to the business, allocates budget for governance programs, and holds Data Owners accountable. Operates at a 12–36 month horizon. Example: The CEO mandates a “single source of truth for customer data” initiative.
T
Tactical Layer — Business Process Managers
Translates strategy into how. Implements governance policies in day-to-day operations, manages Data Stewards, designs workflows, and monitors compliance. Operates at a 1–12 month horizon. Example: The Sales Ops manager standardizes the “customer address” entry process across all CRM forms.
O
Operational Layer — Information Systems Teams
Executes day-to-day. Data Custodians maintain the systems; Data Producers enter and ingest data; Data Consumers use the data and surface issues. Operates at a daily/weekly horizon. Example: A support agent notices that postal codes are being entered without the leading zero; she logs a data quality ticket.

04 Controls & Data Flow Across Systems
Definition
Data Control
A mechanism — technical or procedural — that enforces a governance rule. Controls ensure data is used, stored, and transmitted in accordance with policy. Without controls, policies are just documents.

Controls are categorized by when they act in the data lifecycle:

🛡️ Preventive Controls
Stop bad data from entering the system in the first place.
  • Input validation on forms (required fields, format checks)
  • API schema enforcement (reject malformed payloads)
  • Database constraints (NOT NULL, UNIQUE, FOREIGN KEY)
🔍 Detective Controls
Find data quality issues that have already occurred.
  • Automated profiling jobs (check null rates, outliers daily)
  • Reconciliation reports (compare source vs. target record counts)
  • Anomaly alerts (order amount 10x above average)
🔧 Corrective Controls
Fix issues once detected.
  • Data cleansing pipelines (standardize phone number formats)
  • Manual review queues (human review for flagged records)
  • Re-ingestion workflows (re-pull from source after fix)
How Data Should Flow Across Systems

Data does not live in one place. It moves from source systems through processing layers to consumption tools. Governance must be applied at each stage, not just at the end.

Source Systems
CRM, ERP, IoT, Forms
→
Ingestion Layer
ETL / ELT, Kafka, APIs
→
Processing & Storage
Warehouse, Lake, Lakehouse
→
Consumption Layer
BI, ML, Reports, APIs
→
End Users
Analysts, Apps, Customers
Key Insight — Process + Technology Together
Process without technology means governance rules exist on paper but cannot scale — humans checking data manually cannot keep up with millions of records per day.

Technology without process means tools are running but nobody owns the alerts, nobody fixes the issues, and nobody updates the rules as requirements change.

Sustainable governance requires both, maintained together. Review your controls and your tooling on the same cadence.
05 Data Completeness
Definition
Data Completeness
The degree to which all required data values are present. A field is complete if it contains a value; an entity is complete if all mandatory fields are populated. Completeness is not binary across a dataset — different fields have different completeness requirements.

A critical governance decision is deciding which fields must be complete and which can tolerate gaps. Over-requiring completeness blocks operations (e.g., refusing to process an order because a middle name is missing). Under-requiring it breaks analytics and compliance.

Must Be Complete
Fields that drive business-critical processes or legal obligations.
  • Transaction IDs, invoice numbers
  • Customer name and primary contact
  • Financial amounts (revenue, tax)
  • PII required for compliance (SSN for KYC)
  • Foreign keys that link records
Partial Completeness OK
Optional enrichment fields that improve but do not block operations.
  • Secondary phone number or email
  • Middle name, preferred name
  • Customer industry or company size
  • Product tags and metadata labels
  • UTM marketing attribution fields
Handling Missing Data
When required data is absent, you must decide:
  • Reject: block the record from entering the system
  • Flag: allow but mark for review
  • Default: substitute a known safe default value
  • Impute: estimate from related data (use sparingly)
  • Solicit: trigger a workflow to collect the missing value
Example — E-commerce Order Processing
RetailCorp orders pipeline
  • order_id, customer_id, order_amount: must be complete — pipeline rejects records missing these
  • shipping_address line 2: optional — NULL is valid; delivery uses line 1 only
  • promo_code: partial — NULL means no promo applied; not an error
  • Missing order_amount: record flagged, routed to manual review queue, order held until resolved
06 Validation Rules

Validation rules are the automated checks that enforce data quality at the point of entry or during processing. They are the technical implementation of your completeness and quality policies. A good rule is specific, testable, and tied to a clear business requirement.

Rule Categories with Scenarios
R
Range & Bound Checks
Values must fall within a defined minimum and maximum.
Scenarios: customer age must be 0–120 (reject −5 or 200); order amount must be > $0.00; sensor temperature must be −40°C to 85°C; discount percentage must be 0–100; inventory quantity must be ≥ 0.
F
Format & Pattern Checks
Values must match an expected pattern or structure.
Scenarios: email must match x@x.x regex; US phone must be exactly 10 digits; ZIP code must be 5 or 9 digits; date must be YYYY-MM-DD; product SKU must start with “SKU-” followed by 8 digits.
I
Referential Integrity
A value in one table must exist in another table.
Scenarios: customer_id in Orders must exist in Customers; product_id in OrderItems must exist in Products; warehouse_id in Shipments must exist in Warehouses. Violations indicate orphaned records.
U
Uniqueness Constraints
Certain fields must be unique across the dataset.
Scenarios: transaction IDs must be unique system-wide; email addresses must be unique in the Customers table (one account per email); national ID numbers must be unique per person; product barcodes must be unique per product.
T
Timeliness & Temporal Checks
Timestamps must make logical sense relative to each other or to “now”.
Scenarios: order_date cannot be in the future; ship_date must be after order_date; records with created_at older than 7 years are automatically flagged for retention review; event timestamps cannot pre-date the system go-live date.
B
Business Rule Checks
Rules that encode business logic, often cross-field.
Scenarios: if order_status = "Shipped" then ship_date cannot be NULL; if payment_method = "Credit" then credit_limit must be > order_amount; a refund cannot exceed the original order amount; an employee hire date cannot pre-date the company founding date.
Common Mistake
Defining validation rules once at system launch and never updating them. As business requirements change — new markets, new products, new regulations — your validation rules must be versioned and reviewed. A rule for domestic-only ZIP codes breaks the moment you go international.
07 Data Governance Capability Model

The Data Governance Capability Model (based on DAMA-DMBOK) describes the capabilities an organization builds as it matures. Think of it as levels in a video game — you cannot skip levels, and higher levels require lower ones to be stable first.

1
Core Data Management
The baseline. Data exists, is stored, and can be retrieved. Without this nothing else works.
Data storage & backup Basic cataloging Access provisioning Schema management
2
Master Data Management (MDM)
Establish golden records — a single, authoritative, reconciled version of key business entities (customer, product, supplier). When the CRM says a customer is “Alice Smith” and the ERP says “A. Smith,” MDM resolves which is canonical.
Golden record creation Entity resolution Deduplication Cross-system sync
3
Data Quality Management
Systematic profiling, cleansing, and ongoing monitoring of data quality. Quality is measured, tracked over time, and subject to SLAs.
Data profiling Cleansing pipelines Quality scorecards Anomaly alerting
4
Metadata Management
All three metadata types are tracked: technical (schema, lineage), business (definitions, owners), and operational (access logs, freshness). The data catalog is the primary tool.
Business glossary Data lineage Operational logs Data catalog
5
Full Data Governance
Policies, stewardship, accountability, and continuous improvement running as a formal program. Governance is embedded in culture, not bolted on as a checklist.
Governance council Stewardship program Compliance automation Continuous improvement
Example — Master Data & Golden Records
RetailCorp customer entity
CRM has: Alice Smith, alice@retailcorp.com, (555) 101-2020
ERP has: A. Smith, alice.smith@gmail.com, 5551012020
Billing has: Alice S., a.smith@gmail.com, 555-101-2020

MDM’s golden record resolves this to one canonical entry: Alice Smith, primary email alice@retailcorp.com, phone (555) 101-2020, with alternate email tracked as secondary. All downstream systems reference the golden record, not their own copy.
08 Data Quality Assessment

Declaring that data is “of good quality” requires evidence. Quality assessment is the structured process of measuring data against defined dimensions and thresholds. Each dimension answers a different question about the data.

Trust & Quality Requirements
Level of Trust
Not all data sources are equally trustworthy. Assign a trust score based on:
  • Source reliability: was it validated at entry?
  • Recency: how old is the record?
  • Chain of custody: has it passed through many transformations?
  • Provider reputation: internal systems vs. third-party data vendors
Automation Is Non-Negotiable
Manual quality checks do not scale. Automated quality pipelines should:
  • Run on every data load (not just weekly)
  • Produce quality scorecards per dataset
  • Alert stewards when thresholds are breached
  • Log quality metrics for trend analysis over time
Tools: Great Expectations, dbt tests, Monte Carlo, Soda
Missing Values — Acceptable or Not?
This must be a documented decision, not a judgment call at runtime. For each field: define the acceptable NULL rate, what action to take when it is exceeded, and who is notified. A NULL rate that spikes from 2% to 40% overnight is a data incident, not a normal variation — but you can only know that if you defined “normal.”
09 Data Availability
Definition
Data Availability
The property of data being accessible to authorized users when they need it, in the form they need it, with sufficient performance. Availability is not the same as existence. Data can physically exist in a system and still be unavailable — due to access restrictions, processing delays, system downtime, or simply because nobody told the user it was there.
The Core Problem
“I have the data, but not when I want it — then what is the use?”

A sales manager needs yesterday’s order totals before the 8am stand-up. The ETL pipeline runs at 9am. The data exists, but it is unavailable when it creates business value. This is an availability failure, not a storage failure.

Availability failures have distinct causes. Each requires a different fix:

1
Batch Timing Mismatch
Data is processed in overnight or hourly batches, but users need it in near-real-time. Fix: move to streaming ingestion (Kafka, Spark Streaming) or increase batch frequency.
2
Access Restrictions
Data exists but the requester does not have permission. Sometimes this is intentional; often it is because access was never provisioned after an onboarding or role change. Fix: access request workflows with SLA; regular access certification reviews.
3
System Downtime
The database, warehouse, or API is unavailable. Fix: define Recovery Time Objectives (RTO); maintain read replicas; implement failover. RTO = maximum acceptable downtime; RPO = maximum acceptable data loss.
4
Poor Query Performance
Data is there but queries time out or take hours. Fix: indexing strategy, query optimization, materialized views, pre-aggregated summary tables.
5
Unknown Existence
Users do not know the data exists to ask for it. Fix: invest in data catalog and discoverability (covered in Section 10).
10 Knowing & Finding Your Data

A foundational requirement of data sustainment is simply knowing what data you have. In most legacy organizations, this knowledge lives in the heads of a few veteran engineers. When they leave, the organization loses visibility into its own data assets.

Definition
Data Inventory
A complete register of all data assets in the organization: databases, tables, files, APIs, and feeds. The inventory answers “what do we have?”
Definition
Discoverability
The ability of authorized users to find the data they need without requiring help from IT. Discoverability answers “can I find it myself?”

What a Good Data Management System Exposes
Navigable Data Catalog
A mature data management system (OpenMetadata, Collibra, Alation) lets any authorized user browse:
  • Databases and schemas — what systems exist and how they are organized
  • Table and column descriptions — what each field means in business terms
  • Data owners and stewards — who to contact with questions or quality issues
  • Tags and classifications — PII, financial, public, confidential
  • Lineage — where does this data come from? What consumes it?
  • Sample data — a preview of actual values to confirm relevance before querying
Terminology Consistency — The Data Dictionary

The same word can mean different things to different teams. “Customer” in the CRM might mean any account that has ever signed up; in Finance it might mean any account that has paid an invoice. If teams share data without a shared definition, they will draw contradictory conclusions from the same numbers.

Example — Terminology Conflict
Who is a “customer”?
  • Sales says: anyone who has submitted a lead form (1.2M records)
  • Finance says: anyone who has completed at least one paid transaction (84K records)
  • Marketing says: anyone in our email list (2.1M records)
The data dictionary resolves this: “Customer — a legal entity that has executed a signed contract and made at least one payment. Leads and subscribers are tracked separately as Prospects.” Now all three teams use the same number.
11 Redundancy, Data Hierarchy & Aggregation
Avoiding Redundancy

Data redundancy occurs when the same data is stored in multiple places without a controlled synchronization mechanism. Redundancy itself is not always bad — intentional replication (caches, read replicas) can improve performance. Uncontrolled redundancy is the problem: updates in one system do not propagate to others, creating inconsistency.

Example
Customer address in 3 systems
A customer updates their address in the customer portal. The CRM updates automatically. The ERP still has the old address (batch sync is weekly). The shipping system has a third cached version from 6 months ago.

Result: customer receives marketing mail at new address, invoices at old address, and packages at a wrong address entirely. Fix: single master record in MDM; all systems read from it rather than maintaining their own copy.
Data Hierarchy

Data naturally exists at different levels of granularity. The hierarchy goes from raw events at the bottom to aggregated summaries at the top. Each level serves different use cases:

Summary / KPI Layer Quarterly revenue by region — used by executives
↓
Aggregated Layer Daily sales totals by store — used by managers
↓
Processed / Enriched Layer Individual orders with customer info joined — used by analysts
↓
Raw / Event Layer Every click, scan, or transaction as it happened — used by engineers and data scientists
When to Aggregate — When Not To
Use Aggregation When...
  • Building dashboards and executive reports
  • Training ML models on population-level patterns
  • Reducing query cost on very large fact tables
  • Sharing data externally (protect row-level privacy)
  • Comparing trends over time (weekly/monthly rollups)
Do NOT Aggregate When...
  • Individual-level accountability is required (fraud investigation, audit)
  • Regulators require row-level records (SOX, HIPAA)
  • You need to trace a specific transaction or event
  • Diagnosing a data quality issue (aggregates hide anomalies)
  • Users need to drill down from summary to detail
12 Relationships in Data & Repurposing
Finding Relationships Between Datasets

Real business value often comes from combining datasets that were never designed to be combined. Finding relationships between production data and demand data, for example, enables forecasting, replenishment automation, and waste reduction.

Example — Production vs. Demand
Inventory reorder signal
Joining production output data with demand (order) data reveals: when order volume spikes more than 25% above the 30-day average, inventory drops to critical levels within 4 days.

This relationship was invisible when the two datasets lived in separate systems. Once joined, it drives an automated reorder trigger: if demand spike > 25% detected → initiate supplier order automatically.
Can I Use This Data for This Purpose?

Before repurposing a dataset for a new use case, answer three questions:

L
Legal & Ethical Clearance
Was consent obtained for this new purpose? Does a data use agreement cover it? Does GDPR, CCPA, or HIPAA restrict this use? Example: customer purchase history collected for order fulfillment cannot be sold to third-party advertisers without explicit consent.
T
Technical Fitness
Is the data at the right granularity? In the right format? Fresh enough? Accurate enough for the new use case? Example: daily aggregated sales data is fine for trend reports but too coarse for real-time inventory optimization.
G
Governance Approval
Has the Data Owner approved the new use? Is the new use registered in the data catalog? Is access provisioned through proper channels? Any new use of data is a new access request — it should be logged, approved, and auditable.
Adapting to Changing Requirements
Business needs change. New products, new markets, new regulations create new data requirements. When requirements change: update your data models first, then your validation rules, then your access controls. Changing the data without updating governance artifacts is how technical debt accumulates into a governance crisis.
13 Security Standards & Authorization

Data security is the enforcement side of governance. Every access, every transmission, and every stored record must be protected according to the sensitivity of the data and the requirements of applicable regulations.

Encryption — Protecting Data at Rest and in Transit
🔒 At Rest
Data stored in databases, data lakes, or file systems must be encrypted so that physical access to storage media does not expose data.
  • Standard: AES-256 (industry minimum for sensitive data)
  • Key management: AWS KMS, Azure Key Vault, HashiCorp Vault
  • Scope: entire database, specific columns (column-level encryption for PII), or individual files
🔐 In Transit
Data moving between systems (APIs, ETL pipelines, user connections) must be encrypted to prevent interception.
  • Standard: TLS 1.2 minimum; TLS 1.3 preferred
  • Apply to: web traffic, database connections, inter-service API calls, file transfers (SFTP, not FTP)
  • Never: transmit credentials or PII over unencrypted channels
Access Control Models
ModelHow Access Is DefinedBest ForExample
RBAC
Role-Based		 Access defined by the user’s role in the organization Most enterprise systems; predictable job-function access All “Data Analysts” can read the sales table; no one below “Manager” can export PII
ABAC
Attribute-Based		 Access defined by attributes of the user, the data, and the context Fine-grained, dynamic access (region, classification, time-of-day) A “Regional Manager” in EU can only see EU customer records, not US records
DAC
Discretionary		 Data owner grants access directly to individual users Small teams; ad-hoc data sharing A steward grants a specific analyst read access to a single table for a project
MAC
Mandatory		 Access defined by classification labels; users must have matching clearance Highly regulated environments (government, defense, healthcare) Only users with “Confidential” clearance can access “Confidential” labeled datasets
Direct vs. Indirect Authorization
Direct Authorization
Grant a specific user access to a specific resource.
  • GRANT SELECT ON sales TO alice;
  • Simple but does not scale — managing thousands of individual grants is unmanageable
  • Appropriate for one-off, time-limited access (a vendor, a contractor)
Indirect Authorization (Role-Based)
Grant a role access to resources; assign users to roles.
  • GRANT SELECT ON sales TO ROLE analyst;
    GRANT ROLE analyst TO alice;
  • Scales to thousands of users — change the role, update all members at once
  • Standard practice: onboarding = assign roles; offboarding = revoke roles
Principle of Least Privilege
Grant users only the access they need to do their job — nothing more. A data analyst does not need write access to production tables. A business analyst does not need to see raw PII; they need aggregated reports. Regular access certification reviews (quarterly is typical) revoke access that is no longer needed.
14 Organizational Contexts

Governance does not exist in a vacuum. The right approach depends on the organizational context. What works for a cloud-native startup will fail in a 40-year-old financial institution — and vice versa.

L
Legacy Organizations
Characteristics: on-premise systems, siloed data in departmental databases, high technical debt, manual processes, long approval cycles.

Governance challenge: data is scattered across 20+ systems. Nobody has full visibility. The “data experts” are long-tenured employees who carry institutional knowledge in their heads, not in systems.

Winning strategy: start with a data catalog (inventory first, everything else second). Incremental modernization — do not rip-and-replace. Quick wins build executive confidence for larger investments.
C
Cloud-Native Organizations
Characteristics: scalable cloud infrastructure (S3, BigQuery, Snowflake), automation-driven, real-time data processing, fast iteration cycles, microservices.

Governance challenge: speed creates shadow IT — teams spin up their own data stores without informing governance. Tool proliferation (five data tools added in a quarter). Data lineage is complex across many services.

Winning strategy: enforce governance at the CI/CD pipeline level (governance-as-code). Use native platform tools (e.g., BigQuery’s built-in data catalog, Snowflake’s access policies). Every new data asset must be registered before it goes to production.
R
Highly Regulated Industries
Characteristics: healthcare (HIPAA), financial services (SOX, PCI-DSS), government (FedRAMP), retail (CCPA, GDPR). Compliance is a legal obligation, not a choice.

Governance challenge: compliance fatigue — teams spend so much time on compliance checkboxes that proactive governance suffers. Audit burden is high. Over-restriction can impede legitimate analytics.

Winning strategy: automate compliance checks so they do not require manual effort. Treat regulation as the floor, not the ceiling — good governance exceeds compliance requirements. Use data classification to apply the right controls to the right data.
Example — Same Problem, Different Solutions
“We need a customer 360 view”
  • Legacy bank: run a 6-month data inventory project first; manually document the 12 systems that hold customer data; then build MDM golden records; governance council approval required at each stage.
  • Cloud-native fintech: deploy a data catalog connected to existing Snowflake and S3; tag all existing tables in sprint 1; automatically detect new tables and alert stewards; golden records built in dbt.
  • Hospital network: HIPAA-compliant data warehouse with column-level encryption on all PHI; access controlled by ABAC (role + department + patient relationship); every query logged for audit.
15 Governance Challenges & Winning Strategies
Common Challenges
🚧 Resistance to Change
Teams protect their data domains. “That’s my data” thinking blocks sharing and standardization. People fear governance as extra bureaucracy rather than a tool that makes their work easier. Root cause: governance was imposed top-down without explaining the “why.”
🤷 Lack of Role Clarity
Everyone assumes someone else is responsible for data quality. Issues slip through because no single person owns them. RACI matrices exist on paper but are never enforced. Root cause: governance roles are defined but not embedded in job descriptions, performance reviews, or onboarding.
🧩 Tool Fragmentation
Three data catalogs. Two quality tools. Four BI platforms. Nobody knows which is the system of record. Teams work in silos because their tools do not talk to each other. Root cause: tool procurement without a data platform strategy.
Strategies That Work
1
Executive Sponsorship
A CDO or C-suite champion is the single most reliable predictor of governance program success. Without it, governance stalls at the first departmental pushback. The sponsor sets the tone: “data quality is everyone’s job, and it is tracked in your performance review.”
2
Clear Accountability — RACI per Domain
For every data domain, define who is Responsible, Accountable, Consulted, and Informed. Publish it. Review it quarterly. Every data quality incident should be traceable to a person, not “the system.”
3
Continuous Improvement, Not a Project
Governance is a program, not a one-time implementation. Set quarterly governance OKRs. Run retrospectives when incidents occur. Celebrate improvements in quality scores. Treat governance maturity the same way engineering teams treat technical debt: never fully resolved, always being reduced.
4
Show Early Value
Pick a high-visibility, high-pain data problem (duplicate customers, broken dashboards) and fix it visibly using governance processes. When leadership sees a real problem solved, funding and buy-in follow naturally. “Governance fixed our customer duplicate problem and saved the sales team 3 hours per week” is more persuasive than any governance framework deck.
16 Data Administration for Sustainment

Data Administration is the ongoing operational function that keeps data systems healthy, governed, and aligned with business needs. It spans four stages of data’s life in an organization.

1
Data Creation — Quality at the Source
The cheapest place to fix a data quality problem is before it enters the system. Enforce validation rules at the input layer (web forms, APIs, ETL entry points). Standardize formats across all intake channels — one phone format, one date format. Train data producers on quality standards. Principle: garbage in, garbage out. Fix it upstream.
2
Data Storage — Secure, Available, Recoverable
Storage administration covers three concerns:
  • Secure storage systems: encryption at rest; hardware-level controls for on-prem; cloud storage class selection (e.g., S3 with server-side encryption)
  • Access controls: least privilege on storage objects; bucket/container policies; audit logging of all read/write operations
  • Backup and recovery: define RPO and RTO per system; test restores regularly (not just when disaster strikes); geo-redundant backups for critical data
3
Schema & Metadata Administration
Database schemas change constantly. Schema changes without governance create downstream breakage. Administration tasks: version-controlled schema migrations (Flyway, Liquibase); impact analysis before any structural change; updating catalog metadata to match schema changes; communicating breaking changes to data consumers with appropriate lead time.
4
User & Access Administration
Provisioning and deprovisioning access is a continuous process. Best practices: automated onboarding (new employee = automatic role assignment); offboarding SLA (access revoked within 24 hours of departure); quarterly access certification (managers confirm each employee still needs their access); service account inventory (know what each system account can do).
17 Data Lifecycle Risk Points

Every stage of the data lifecycle introduces its own category of risk. Sustainable governance means understanding where things can go wrong and placing controls at each risk point — not just at storage or at deletion.

Stage 1 — Creation
📝
Poor Quality at Entry
  • Typos, missing values, wrong formats
  • Inconsistent data entry standards
  • Untested API integrations injecting bad records
  • No validation at the intake point

Controls: input validation, required field enforcement, format standardization, schema-on-write
Stage 2 — Storage
🗄️
Breaches & Data Loss
  • Unencrypted sensitive data
  • Overly permissive access controls
  • Misconfigured cloud storage (public S3 buckets)
  • No backup or untested recovery

Controls: encryption at rest, least-privilege access, backup + recovery testing, storage configuration audits
Stage 3 — Usage
📊
Misuse & Over-Exposure
  • Data used outside its consented purpose
  • PII exposed to unauthorized users
  • No audit trail for who queried what
  • Unmasked data in non-production environments

Controls: query logging, column-level security, data masking in dev/test, purpose registration
Stage 4 — Deletion
🗑️
Non-Compliance
  • Deleting data that must be retained (SOX: 7 years)
  • Failing to delete data past its retention period (GDPR right to erasure)
  • Incomplete deletion (backups still contain the record)
  • No record of what was deleted or when

Controls: retention schedules, automated archival, deletion audit logs, legal hold workflows

Closing Thought — Sustainment as a Mindset
Data sustainment is not a checklist. It is a mindset embedded in every team that creates, moves, stores, uses, or deletes data. Planning prevents fire-fighting. Policies create consistency. Controls catch failures early. And a culture of ownership — where every person in the organization understands their hat and wears it — is what makes the whole system self-sustaining over time.
