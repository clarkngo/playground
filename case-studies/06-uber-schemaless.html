The Growth Explosion

In 2013, Uber was experiencing the kind of growth that breaks infrastructure. The company was expanding into new cities at a blistering pace, and each new city meant a multiplicative increase in trip volume. Rides were doubling on a near-monthly basis, and the engineering team found themselves in a race against their own success.

At the heart of Uber is the trip record. Every ride generates a complex data object: pickup location, dropoff location, route taken, fare calculation, driver information, rider details, payment processing, surge pricing data, and more. These records needed to be written reliably, quickly, and at enormous scale. Losing even a single trip record could mean a lost payment or a billing dispute.

The engineering team knew their existing PostgreSQL-based storage would not survive the year. They needed a plan, and they needed it fast.

PostgreSQL Hits the Wall

Uber had been running on PostgreSQL, a rock-solid relational database beloved by engineers everywhere. But PostgreSQL was designed for vertical scaling: you make the server bigger, you optimize queries, you add read replicas. Horizontal scaling, splitting data across multiple independent database servers, was not a first-class feature.

The trip database was generating write volumes that a single PostgreSQL primary could not absorb. Write-ahead log shipping to replicas introduced lag. Schema migrations on tables with hundreds of millions of rows took hours and required maintenance windows. Adding columns to the trip table was becoming a multi-day project.

Uber tried to shard PostgreSQL manually, but the operational complexity was enormous. Each shard needed its own backup strategy, its own monitoring, its own failover plan. The team was spending more time managing the database than building features.

Evaluating the Alternatives

The team conducted a thorough evaluation of existing distributed databases. Cassandra was the first candidate, known for its write-heavy performance and linear scalability. However, Cassandra brought its own operational challenges: compaction storms could spike latency unpredictably, the data model required careful denormalization, and the team had limited operational experience with it.

HBase was also considered for its strong consistency guarantees and integration with the Hadoop ecosystem. But HBase had a steep operational learning curve and depended on HDFS and ZooKeeper, adding complexity to the stack. The Java garbage collection pauses were also a concern for latency-sensitive trip writes.

After weeks of evaluation, a radical idea emerged from the team: what if they built their own storage system, but did it in the most pragmatic way possible? Instead of building a database from scratch, they could build a thin layer on top of something they already understood deeply: MySQL.

Designing Schemaless

The design of Schemaless was elegant in its simplicity. Each record (called a "cell") consisted of just four fields: a row UUID, a column name, a JSON blob, and a monotonically increasing version number. There were no schemas, no ALTER TABLE statements, no migration scripts. If you wanted to add a new field, you just started writing it in the JSON blob.

The append-only design was a deliberate choice. When a trip record needed updating (say, the fare was recalculated), Schemaless would write a new version of the cell rather than updating in place. This meant writes never blocked on reads, historical data was always preserved, and debugging billing disputes became trivial because you could see every version of a trip record.

Underneath, Schemaless used MySQL as its storage engine. Each MySQL instance held a set of shards, and a routing layer directed reads and writes to the correct shard based on the row UUID. MySQL handled the hard problems of data durability, replication, and crash recovery. Schemaless handled the hard problems of sharding, schema flexibility, and write ordering.

The Cell Architecture

One of the most innovative aspects of Schemaless was its cell-based architecture for geographic isolation. Uber operated a separate Schemaless "cell" (a complete, independent cluster) for each major geographic region. A trip in San Francisco would be stored in the US West cell. A trip in London would be stored in the Europe cell.

This cell architecture had enormous advantages. A failure in one cell could not cascade to another. Latency was kept low because data was stored close to where it was generated. Compliance with data residency regulations became straightforward because data physically stayed within its region.

Each cell was fully self-contained with its own set of MySQL shards, its own Schemaless API servers, and its own async workers that built secondary indexes. If the Europe cell went down, rides in the US were completely unaffected.

Scaling to Millions

By 2015, Schemaless was handling the vast majority of trip storage. The migration from PostgreSQL was done incrementally, with a dual-write strategy: new trips were written to both PostgreSQL and Schemaless, with Schemaless becoming the source of truth once the team was confident in its reliability.

The async worker system handled secondary indexing. When a new trip cell was written, workers would pick up the change from a changelog and update any secondary indexes (such as looking up trips by rider ID or driver ID). This kept the write path fast and simple while still allowing flexible querying.

By 2016, Schemaless was handling millions of trips per day across multiple geographic cells. Write latency remained under 5 milliseconds. The system had proven itself through multiple traffic spikes, including New Year's Eve surges that pushed traffic to many times the normal volume.

Build vs. Buy: The Aftermath

Building Schemaless was not without cost. The team had to maintain a custom storage system, train new engineers on its internals, and build operational tooling from scratch. When a MySQL shard needed to be split, the team had to handle the resharding process themselves.

But the decision paid enormous dividends. By building on MySQL, Uber leveraged decades of MySQL operational knowledge. DBAs could use familiar tools for backups and monitoring. MySQL replication provided a proven mechanism for data durability. And the thin Schemaless layer was simple enough that the entire team could understand and debug it.

Schemaless went on to store not just trip data but also other high-write-volume datasets at Uber. It became a foundational piece of infrastructure that supported the company through its period of hypergrowth, proving that sometimes the best database is the one you build yourself, on top of something you trust.

Lessons Extracted

Build on what you trust.

By using MySQL as the storage engine, Uber avoided building the hardest parts of a database (durability, replication, crash recovery) and leveraged decades of MySQL operational tooling.

Append-only simplifies everything.

Never updating or deleting data eliminated entire categories of bugs: lost updates, write conflicts, and data corruption. Every version of every record was preserved forever.

Geographic isolation prevents cascading failures.

The cell architecture meant that a failure in one region could never affect another. This was critical for a service where downtime meant stranded riders.

Schema flexibility enables velocity.

By storing data as JSON blobs, teams could add new fields without migrations. This removed a major bottleneck in the development process.

Async secondary indexing keeps the write path fast.

By building indexes asynchronously, the critical write path remained simple and fast, while still supporting flexible queries for analytics and operations.

Key Takeaways

Sometimes the best database is a thin layer on top of a database you already understand deeply.
Append-only, immutable data models trade storage cost for simplicity, debuggability, and reliability.
Geographic cell isolation is a powerful pattern for global-scale systems that need fault boundaries.
Evaluate existing solutions thoroughly, but do not be afraid to build when your requirements are truly unique.
