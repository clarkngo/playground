Why this workshop matters
🎯 Workshop Goal
Plan and deliver a workshop or presentation on AI literacy, implications, or applications — tailored for a Seattle City audience navigating the City's own AI policies and workforce skilling effort. This page extracts relevant intelligence to sharpen the workshop design.
What Seattle has already signaled (from the 2025–2026 AI Plan)
"With so many AI technologies available and innumerable vendors pushing to sell to the City, we face an urgent need to equip its leaders, managers, and employees with foundational knowledge to navigate this evolving landscape responsibly."
— City of Seattle 2025–2026 AI Plan, Pillar 3: Workforce Upskilling and Capacity Building
Extracted signals — what the City is prioritizing
Signal Detail from Plan Workshop Implication
Fact vs. Fiction
AI Fundamentals tier
 All staff must distinguish fact from fiction and understand core AI concepts before applying them. Open with myth-busting and grounding exercises — don't assume any baseline.
Human Decision-Making
Required by policy
 Plan explicitly calls out "required human decision-making" as a competency all staff need. Frame AI as decision-support, not decision-replacement. Include scenarios where human override is mandatory.
Shared Terminology
Cross-dept alignment
 The City wants one common language across departments to avoid siloed or conflicting AI efforts. Provide a glossary handout. Anchor all activities to the City's own vocabulary.
Ethics: Bias & Privacy
Trust and equity
 "Recognize ethical considerations such as bias and privacy" and "uphold community trust." Include a dedicated segment on bias in public-sector AI — real examples from government use cases.
Data Safeguarding
Sensitive data
 Staff must know how to "safeguard sensitive data and ensure equity." Cover data classification, what NOT to put in AI tools, and approved platforms.
Approved Platforms
Technologist tier
 Technologists need to know "what technologies and platforms the City has approved." If audience includes tech staff — cover the approved toolchain and where to find contracting standards.
Community of Practice
Ongoing learning
 Training connects staff to "communities of practice" for shared learning post-training. End with a call-to-action: point attendees to existing City CoPs or offer to start one.
Three training tiers the City is building toward
📘 AI Fundamentals
For everyone — officials, managers, front-line staff. Fact vs. fiction, core concepts, human oversight, shared terminology. The "informed caution" baseline.
⚙️ AI Approaches
For decision-makers and direct users. How to match problems to AI technologies, apply correct models, and engage security/privacy/ethics review teams.
🔧 AI Solutions
For technologists and operational staff. What platforms are approved, where to find technical and contracting standards, how to participate in expert forums.
Audience segments identified in the City's plan
🏛️
Officials & Leaders
Department heads, elected officials, senior directors
  • Understand strategic AI landscape without hype
  • Know when to approve vs. pause an AI initiative
  • Ask the right accountability questions
  • Recognize bias and equity risks at a policy level
📋
Managers & Supervisors
Program managers, team leads, operations supervisors
  • Match workflow problems to AI approaches
  • Know which vendor claims to trust — and how to verify
  • Understand privacy and data sensitivity for their team
  • Maintain human oversight in automated workflows
👷
Front-line Employees
Service staff, analysts, clerks, field workers
  • Use AI tools confidently and safely in daily work
  • Know what data they can and cannot put into AI systems
  • Recognize when AI output needs human review
  • Raise concerns through the right channels
💻
Technologists & IT Staff
Developers, data engineers, IT operations, architects
  • Know the approved AI platforms and their constraints
  • Apply City contracting and security standards
  • Participate in expert forums and share lessons learned
  • Run ethical and privacy reviews before deployment
Who is likely in the room for our workshop?
Which tier(s) will we be presenting to?
The City plan covers all four, but our workshop probably targets one or two. Knowing this drives everything else — content depth, tone, hands-on vs. lecture, jargon level.
What's the mix of technical vs. non-technical attendees?
A mixed room needs bridging analogies. A homogeneous room can go deeper on one tier faster.
What departments are represented?
Seattle City has many departments with very different AI exposure — SPD, SDOT, SPU, Seattle IT, Finance, etc. Relevant examples shift significantly.
Core content themes — derived from Seattle AI Plan + Responsible AI policy
🧠 AI Fundamentals
What AI is and isn't. Generative AI vs. predictive AI vs. automation. Where the hype ends and the reality begins. No jargon required.
⚖️ Responsible AI
Bias, fairness, and equity in government algorithms. Privacy by design. When AI output must be reviewed by a human before action is taken.
🔐 Data Safety
What counts as sensitive data in a public-sector context. What you can and cannot feed into AI tools. Approved platforms vs. shadow AI.
🤝 Human + AI Collaboration
AI as decision support, not decision-making. The human-in-the-loop principle. How to verify AI output before acting on it.
🏙️ Government AI Use Cases
Real examples from city governments using AI — permit processing, 311 routing, traffic prediction, public records, translation. What worked, what failed.
🚦 Procurement & Vendor Risk
How to evaluate AI vendor claims. Red flags in vendor pitches. Seattle's AI policy requirements that vendors must meet. Avoiding vendor lock-in.
🌐 Community Impact & Equity
AI effects on Seattle's diverse communities. Historical examples of algorithmic harm in public services. Designing for equity from the start.
🔭 What's Coming
Near-term AI trends relevant to city government. Agentic AI and automation pipelines. What to watch for in the next 12–18 months.

Format options to evaluate
⚡ Lightning Talk (30–45 min) Low commitment · High reach
A fast-paced presentation covering the most essential AI literacy concepts. Works well as a conference session, all-hands segment, or lunch-and-learn. No hands-on component.
  • 1Hook: surprising AI stat or recent government AI story (5 min)
  • 2What AI is and isn't — myth-busting (10 min)
  • 3Seattle AI policy overview — what the City has committed to (10 min)
  • 4Practical rules: what to do and not do with AI at work (10 min)
  • 5Q&A + resources handout (10 min)
🏫 Half-Day Workshop (3–4 hrs) Medium commitment · Deep learning
Covers fundamentals through hands-on application. Small group exercises, scenario analysis, and role-specific breakouts. Aligned with the City's AI Fundamentals + AI Approaches tiers.
  • 1Module 1: AI Fundamentals — concepts, terms, myth-busting (45 min)
  • 2Module 2: Responsible AI — bias, privacy, equity, human oversight (45 min)
  • 3Break
  • 4Module 3: Hands-on — evaluate real AI outputs, spot issues (45 min)
  • 5Module 4: Role-specific breakouts — what does this mean for my job? (30 min)
  • 6Module 5: Seattle policy + what's next — CoP, resources, next steps (30 min)
🎮 Scenario-Based Workshop (2 hrs) Engaging · Decision-focused
Participants work through 3–4 realistic government AI scenarios in small teams: evaluate a vendor pitch, review an AI-generated decision, respond to a bias complaint, handle a data leak incident. Discussion-heavy, minimal lecture.
  • 1Brief framing: key principles participants will need (20 min)
  • 2Scenario 1: Evaluating an AI vendor's pitch for your department (25 min)
  • 3Scenario 2: An AI system flagged a resident's benefits application — what now? (25 min)
  • 4Scenario 3: A staff member used a non-approved AI tool on sensitive data (20 min)
  • 5Debrief + policy alignment + resources (20 min)
🔁 Learning Series (4–6 sessions × 1 hr) High commitment · Sustained change
A modular, recurring series — one topic per session — that builds capability over weeks. Best for a team or department that wants genuine transformation, not just awareness. Aligns with all three City training tiers.
  • 1Session 1: What is AI? (Fundamentals, myths, terminology)
  • 2Session 2: Responsible AI and equity in government
  • 3Session 3: Data safety and approved tools
  • 4Session 4: AI use cases for your department (hands-on)
  • 5Session 5: Procurement and vendor evaluation
  • 6Session 6: What's next — trends, policy, community of practice
Questions to resolve before finalizing the workshop
About the audience
Who specifically is in the room — and what departments are they from?
Seattle City has 30+ departments with very different AI exposure. The right examples for SPD are very different from Finance or SDOT.
What's their current baseline? Have they had any AI training before?
If they have, we skip the basics and go deeper. If not, we budget more time for fundamentals.
Are attendees volunteers or required to attend?
Motivation and engagement strategy differ significantly. Mandatory training needs more interactive design to maintain attention.
About the format
How long do we have — and is it one session or recurring?
30 min, 2 hrs, and half-day each require very different designs. A recurring series requires different commitment from the organizer.
In-person, virtual, or hybrid?
Hands-on exercises and small-group work are much harder in hybrid. If hybrid, we need facilitation tools (Miro, Slido, Mentimeter, etc.).
Should we use the City's own AI policy documents as workshop material?
Using official Seattle AI policy text could increase buy-in and institutional credibility — but needs legal/comms clearance to reproduce in materials.
About the outcome
What do we want participants to be able to DO after the workshop?
The answer drives everything else. "Know more about AI" is too vague. "Identify when an AI vendor claim needs scrutiny" is concrete and testable.
Is there a follow-on action — a CoP, a pilot project, a policy process?
A workshop with a clear next step retains learning much better than a standalone event. What can we plug participants into?
Are we connected to the City's CTAB or the official AI skilling program?
The Community Technology Advisory Board (CTAB) and the City's own workforce skilling program are already in motion. Coordinating (rather than duplicating) adds more value.
