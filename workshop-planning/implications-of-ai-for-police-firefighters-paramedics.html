Why this workshop matters
🎯 Workshop Goal
Plan and deliver a workshop on the real implications of AI for police officers, firefighters, and paramedics — grounded in the tools already entering their workflows (Code Four, Longeye, Prepared), the legal constraints they operate under (CJIS, HIPAA, King County prosecution standards), and the Seattle IT policy framework. The goal is to move first responders from AI-curious to AI-competent: able to use AI safely, catch its failures, and push back on deployments that create risk.
What Seattle has already signaled (from the 2025–2026 AI Plan)
"With so many AI technologies available and innumerable vendors pushing to sell to the City, we face an urgent need to equip its leaders, managers, and employees with foundational knowledge to navigate this evolving landscape responsibly."

Extracted signals — what the City is prioritizing
Signal Detail from Plan Workshop Implication
Fact vs. Fiction
AI Fundamentals tier
 All staff must distinguish fact from fiction and understand core AI concepts before applying them. Open with a real hallucination from a deployed police tool (Code Four frog incident). Ground AI concepts in what responders have already heard about — not abstract demos.
Human Decision-Making
Required by policy
 Plan explicitly calls out "required human decision-making" as a competency all staff need. Frame around the specific moments first responders own: signing a report, clearing a scene, dispatching a unit. AI prepares the draft; the officer, firefighter, or paramedic owns the decision.
Shared Terminology
Cross-dept alignment
 The City wants one common language across departments to avoid siloed or conflicting AI efforts. Bridge Seattle IT terms (hallucination, human-in-the-loop) to field language: a "hallucination" is an invented fact in a report you are about to sign. Use CJIS and HIPAA as the shared privacy anchors across SPD, SFD, and EMS.
Ethics: Bias & Privacy
Trust and equity
 "Recognize ethical considerations such as bias and privacy" and "uphold community trust." Use the predictive patrol feedback-loop scenario (Scenario 4 in the Activities tab). Connect algorithmic bias directly to community trust in the neighborhoods SPD and SFD serve.
Data Safeguarding
Sensitive data
 Staff must know how to "safeguard sensitive data and ensure equity." Run a concrete drill: which of these five inputs can go into a public AI tool? (Incident address, suspect name, NCIC hit, 911 caller voice, anonymous tip.) CJIS and HIPAA are the red lines, not general caution.
Approved Platforms
Technologist tier
 Technologists need to know "what technologies and platforms the City has approved." Show responders the difference between a CJIS-compliant private cloud instance (Code Four, Axon) and a public consumer tool (ChatGPT, Copilot on a personal device). One is a policy violation; the other is approved workflow.
Community of Practice
Ongoing learning
 Training connects staff to "communities of practice" for shared learning post-training. Close with a clear ask: officers and medics who catch a real hallucination in the field should report it. Build the feedback loop from front-line users back to the university and Seattle IT — not just from top down.

📘 AI Fundamentals
For everyone — officials, managers, front-line staff. Fact vs. fiction, core concepts, human oversight, shared terminology. The "informed caution" baseline.
⚙️ AI Approaches

Room composition questions
Is this a mixed first-responder room or a single-department session?
A mixed SPD/SFD/EMS room benefits from shared scenarios (bias audit, hallucination redline) but needs role-specific breakouts for CJIS vs. HIPAA distinctions. A single-department session can go deeper on their specific tools and legal constraints.
Are patrol officers and command staff attending together?
Command staff need vendor evaluation and policy framing. Patrol needs hands-on redline practice. Mixing them risks losing both — consider parallel tracks or a sequenced agenda that separates policy from operations.
Are any of the tools being discussed already live in attendees' workflows?
If Code Four or a similar tool is already deployed, participants have real examples to bring — and real frustrations. Lead with their experience first. If no tools are live yet, frame as "what is coming and how to prepare."
Core content themes — for police, firefighters, and paramedics
🧠 AI Fundamentals
Generative AI vs. predictive models vs. computer vision — in plain terms. What a hallucination is and why it matters when you are about to sign a report or dispatch a unit. No computer science background required.
⚖️ Responsible AI
How algorithmic bias shows up in predictive policing and fire-risk tools. Why training data from historically over-patrolled neighborhoods produces a feedback loop. What human-in-the-loop means as a legal and ethical obligation, not just a policy phrase.
🔐 Data Safety: CJIS & HIPAA
The hard lines for first responders: CJIS-protected criminal justice data cannot touch public AI tools. HIPAA-covered patient information cannot leave a compliant system. What approved platforms look like versus shadow AI on a personal device.
🤝 Human + AI Collaboration
AI drafts the report; the officer signs it. AI flags the call priority; the dispatcher decides. AI processes the recordings; the detective verifies each lead. The collaboration pattern is the same across all three roles — and so is the legal accountability.
🚔 Public Safety AI Use Cases
Real deployments: Code Four for incident reports, Longeye for call evidence, Prepared for 911 translation. What worked, what hallucinated, what a department in Utah and Redmond learned from going first — and what Seattle should do differently.
🚦 Vendor Evaluation
How to audit a vendor's training data for bias. What questions to demand answers to before approving a predictive patrol or fire-risk tool. Seattle's Privacy Review Process and why it applies even to tools that "just help" with paperwork.
🌐 Community Trust & Equity
How AI errors in police reports affect the communities where SPD operates. How predictive tools can concentrate harm in neighborhoods already burdened by over-policing. Why building trust matters more than efficiency gains that come at equity's expense.
🔭 What's Coming
Agentic AI in dispatch and triage. Body-cam real-time transcription at scale. Predictive wildfire and cardiac-risk modeling. What to watch for in the next 12–18 months — and which deployments to pressure-test before they go live.

Format options to evaluate
⚡ Lightning Talk (30–45 min) Low commitment · High reach · Roll call or briefing room
A fast-paced briefing designed for a shift lineup, pre-shift roll call, or department all-hands. Covers what AI tools are entering the workflow, what the risks are, and the three rules every first responder needs to know before they sign anything AI-drafted.
  • 1Hook: the Code Four frog — what happened and why it matters (5 min)
  • 2What AI does to your words: hallucinations, bias, and invented facts (10 min)
  • 3The three rules: CJIS/HIPAA red lines, human-in-the-loop requirement, safe vs. banned tools (10 min)
  • 4One question every responder should ask before signing an AI draft (5 min)
  • 5Q&A + field card handout (10 min)
🏫 Half-Day Workshop (3–4 hrs) Medium commitment · Deep learning · Station or classroom
Covers fundamentals through hands-on application with role-specific breakouts for police, fire, and EMS. Participants leave with a safe-prompt habit, a bias-audit checklist, and the ability to redline an AI-generated report.
  • 1Module 1: AI in public safety — what is already deployed, what is coming (45 min)
  • 2Module 2: Legal guardrails — CJIS, HIPAA, KCPAO stance on AI evidence (45 min)
  • 3Break
  • 4Module 3: Hands-on redline — find the hallucinations in a real AI-drafted report (45 min)
  • 5Module 4: Role breakouts — Police (report signing), Fire (resource tools), EMS/Dispatch (translation and triage) (30 min)
  • 6Module 5: Safe use framework + next steps — what to do when your department deploys a new tool (30 min)
🎮 Scenario-Based Workshop (2 hrs) Engaging · Decision-focused · Small groups
Participants work through three realistic first-responder AI scenarios in small teams — one for each department track. Discussion-heavy, minimal lecture. Draws directly from the Hands-On Activities scenario bank.
  • 1Brief framing: the three things AI gets wrong in public safety (15 min)
  • 2Scenario A (Police): AI-drafted DUI report — find and fix the hallucinations before signing (30 min)
  • 3Scenario B (EMS/Dispatch): AI-translated 911 call — what did the translation miss, and what does that change? (30 min)
  • 4Scenario C (Command): Vendor pitches a predictive patrol tool — approve, reject, or require audit? (25 min)
  • 5Cross-team debrief: what pattern did all three scenarios share? + policy alignment + field card (20 min)
🔁 Learning Series (5 sessions × 1 hr) High commitment · Sustained capability · Department cohort
A modular series designed for a single department cohort — one session per week over five weeks. Builds from AI literacy to hands-on practice to department-level policy readiness. Best for departments actively evaluating or deploying AI tools.
  • 1Session 1: What AI does and does not do — demystified for first responders
  • 2Session 2: Legal exposure — CJIS, HIPAA, Brady risk, and the AI report problem
  • 3Session 3: Hands-on lab — redline an AI report, fix a bad translation, write a safe prompt
  • 4Session 4: Bias, equity, and community trust — evaluating predictive tools in your department
  • 5Session 5: Your department's AI readiness — vendor checklist, policy gaps, and building the feedback loop

Summary: Pillar 3 - Workforce Upskilling and Capacity Building
Pillar 3 sets a city-wide mandate to move from AI confusion to AI competence through three coordinated training tiers:
• AI Fundamentals (for everyone): shared language, hype-vs-reality grounding, bias/privacy awareness, and human-in-the-loop expectations.
• AI Approaches and Solutions (for decision-makers): matching operational problems to the right tools while passing security and ethical review.
• Technical Implementation (for technologists): approved platforms, contracting standards, and expert collaboration for responsible deployment.
From the Mayor's office to front-line responders, the intent is alignment on how, when, and why Seattle uses AI.
How this workshop implements Pillar 3
1) Operationalizing AI Fundamentals
• Hallucination checks: The Redline exercise trains responders to distinguish fact from fiction under high-stakes constraints.
• Terminology alignment: Context and concepts sections align SPD/SFD language with Seattle IT definitions for Generative AI and Predictive Analytics.
2) Scaling AI Approaches and Solutions
• Problem-to-technology matching: The Traffic Light framework clarifies technical capability versus legally approved city use.
• Ethics and privacy guardrails: Sandbox activities require bias/privacy checks before any field deployment decision.
3) Building Communities of Practice
• University as neutral forum: Cross-department dialogue among SPD, SFD, and EMS on shared CJIS/HIPAA challenges.
• Capacity via Private RAG: Responders practice grounded retrieval on validated local protocols instead of generic public AI responses.
🔧 Implementation Gap-Mender
Pillar 3 defines what must be learned; this workshop provides how to learn it through repeatable, scenario-based practice. The city plan is the blueprint, and the workshop is the power tool that builds field-ready judgment.
✅ Key Takeaway
Close the workshop with a Path to Participation: show responders how to join Seattle's internal AI communities of practice so front-line feedback reaches the technologists building city systems.

EMS/Fire expansion: Hands-on module - The Fire/EMS AI Sandbox
🚑 Why this track is practical now
Focusing on EMS and Fire shifts the discussion from surveillance and evidence into triage, logistics, and life-saving speed. With SFD and King County EMS modernizing data pipelines, this module feels near-future and directly operational.
🎯 Objective
Test AI's ability to augment decision-making during high-stress medical and fire incidents without violating HIPAA or Seattle IT's human-in-the-loop mandate.
Activity A: Radio to PCR Translation Challenge
Goal: Assess whether GenAI can convert chaotic radio patches into structured patient care report narratives.
Input Example: Medic 10 to Harborview. En route with a 45 y/o male, fall from height. GCS 12, heart rate 110, BP 90 over 60. Patient is combative. ETA 5 minutes.
Playground Task: Use a sandbox LLM to generate a formal NEMSIS-style narrative.
Vibe Check: Redline hallucinated details, especially invented fall height or medical history.
Legal Focus: HIPAA compliance and why public AI products are not appropriate for this workflow.
Activity B: Syndromic Surveillance and Resource Deployment
Goal: Use AI pattern detection to support unit deployment during events like Seafair or heatwaves.
Scenario: You have 10 extra peak-time units to place across Seattle.
Data Prompt: A mock AI dashboard flags a rising cluster of heat-related calls in the International District before it reaches critical threshold.
Decision Point: Option 1: deploy by historical busy maps (Belltown/Downtown). Option 2: deploy by emerging AI syndromic trend (International District).
Discussion: Is this predictive response or bias? What if low digital reporting suppresses a true hotspot?
Activity C: Multi-Agent Dispatcher (Tech-Savvy Track)
Goal: Roleplay a multi-agent response for a maritime emergency, such as a ferry fire in Elliott Bay.
• Agent 1 - Logistics: Tracks SFD fireboat locations and tides.
• Agent 2 - Triage: Scans social and 911 text-to-voice feeds for casualty counts.
• Agent 3 - Policy: Flags recommendations that violate Seattle's 2025 AI Plan or mutual aid constraints.
Supervisor Task: Participants act as human-in-the-loop supervisors, choose among competing AI recommendations, and justify using explainability principles.

Hands-on activity section: First Responder AI Sandbox (Seattle Edition)
🧫 Lab framing
This 60-minute laboratory follows an NCyTE-style Playground pattern: technical execution, critical analysis, and policy reflection.
Duration: 60 minutes
Setting: University AI Lab (private sandbox environment) aligned to Seattle's 2025-2026 AI Plan and King County legal restrictions.
🎯 Learning objectives
1) Identify hallucinations in AI-generated reports that can create legal exposure.
2) Map AI outputs to Seattle IT Responsible AI principles for policy compliance.
3) Apply human-in-the-loop prompting techniques to correct AI behavior safely.
Activity 1: Evidence Integrity Redline (Police track)
Focus: KCPAO constraints on AI-generated narratives and evidentiary integrity.
Scenario: DUI stop on 3rd Ave with BWV transcript as the source record.
Raw Data: Subject was weaving. Stopped at 22:14. Officer Miller approached. Subject smells of alcohol. Subject stated I only had two beers at the Mariners game. Officer Miller noted a half-empty can of Rainier on the passenger floor. Subject failed the walk-and-turn.
AI Draft: On October 14, 2025, at approximately 10:14 PM, Officer Miller initiated a traffic stop on a blue sedan for reckless driving. Upon contact, the driver admitted to heavy drinking at a sporting event. Officer Miller observed multiple open containers in the vehicle. The suspect was unable to stand during field sobriety tests.
Hands-on Task 1: Redline every claim in the draft not explicitly supported by source transcript details.
Hands-on Task 2: Brady risk discussion: how defense counsel can use small hallucinations to impeach testimony.
Activity 2: Structured Triage Refiner (EMS/Fire track)
Focus: HIPAA-safe transformation of chaotic caller language into usable medical structure.
Task flow: Submit raw transcript, then apply a refinement prompt requesting MIST format and explicit exclusion of PII.
Refinement Prompt: Extract only medical vitals and patient history. Format into MIST. Do not include names or addresses to maintain HIPAA compliance.
Verification: Cross-check output against King County EMS Blue Book RAG reference, including Narcan dosing alignment with Seattle 2026 protocol guidance.
Activity 3: Bias and Equity Policy Map (Leadership track)
Focus: Pillar 3 capacity building through policy-aware audit decisions.
Scenario: Vendor proposes a predictive fire-risk model for Rainier Valley building prioritization.
Audit Checklist: Transparency of scoring logic, equity impact across housing types, and deployment risk profile.
Decision Output: Write a two-sentence recommendation: Approve for Pilot or Reject for Bias Risk.
Lab deliverables: The checkout
1) Annotated report with hallucinations clearly marked.
2) One-sentence safety system prompt to reduce legal-risk outputs.
3) Reflection prompt: What is one task in my daily shift that AI should never touch?

Workshop proposal
🛡️ The Safe-Vibe Public Safety Lab
Goal: Transition Seattle first responders from AI-curious to AI-competent using a university-governed sandbox that pairs technical depth with legal and policy guardrails.
1. Existing workshops and competitor landscape
To lead, we improve on a vendor-heavy training landscape:
• Vector Solutions (April 2026): Fire service readiness and VoiceComplete transcription focus.
• UW Emergency Management (2026): Strong on ICS/FEMA drills; opportunity to add Generative AI agents curriculum for patrol and EMS.
• AI2 Incubator (Seattle): Community innovation hackathons skew toward permitting and civic tech; our niche is high-stakes operational public safety (police and fire).
2. Case studies (why now)
• Seattle Fire / UW Medicine (2026): Automated pipeline linking 911 dispatch to hospital outcomes exemplifies data excellence from the Mayor AI plan narrative.
• Truleo / Axon shift: Agencies such as Butte County report cutting report-writing time from about 40 minutes to about 7 minutes with AI-assisted workflows.
• Seattle surveillance pause (March 2026): Mayor Wilson paused certain surveillance expansions over privacy and ICE-related concerns, underscoring the need for privacy-by-design AI education hosted outside vendor sales cycles.
2a. Real-world tools in deployment
📋 Code Four — AI report writing (Utah)
Deployed by the Heber City Police Department in Utah. Officers use Code Four to transcribe and draft incident reports from body-worn camera audio, cutting manual report writing from 1–2 hours down to minutes at approximately $30 per officer per month.
• What works: Significant time savings free officers for field work instead of paperwork.
• Cautionary example: In one incident, background audio was misinterpreted and the AI reported that an officer had shape-shifted into a frog — a hallucination caused by ambient noise in the recording.
• Key takeaway: Human correction is still required on every output. The tool assists; it does not replace officer judgment. FOX 13 coverage
🔍 Longeye — Investigative AI (Redmond, WA)
Redmond Police used Longeye to process evidence in an active case investigation.
• Impact: The tool processed 60 hours of recorded phone calls in a matter of minutes — work that would have taken investigators days or weeks manually.
• Key takeaway: AI dramatically accelerates evidence review at scale, enabling investigators to focus on judgment calls rather than playback. A strong case for AI in the analytical tier (green-light use).
🚨 Prepared — AI in emergency call handling
An AI-assisted platform designed for 911 and emergency dispatch workflows, with three distinct use cases:
• Notes vs. transcription: Dispatchers can compare their own call notes against the AI-transcribed record to find anything they may have missed during a high-stress call.
• Language access: Provides real-time AI translation on-call, eliminating the need to wait for a human interpreter during time-critical incidents.
• Hold time reduction: AI handles initial triage during surge periods to reduce long call hold times.

2b. Risks we cannot ignore
⚠️ AI can reinforce existing bias
AI systems trained on historical data inherit the patterns and prejudices in that data. In public safety, this means predictive tools can concentrate enforcement in already over-policed communities, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop. Bias is not a hypothetical — it is a documented outcome in deployed systems.
⚖️ AI-generated reports pose high-stakes legal risks
A June 2025 report from the Fair and Just Prosecution project found that AI-generated police reports introduce factual inaccuracies, insert implicit bias, and can be used by defense counsel to impeach officer testimony — even when the officer was unaware of the AI error.
• Hallucinations in legal records are not just embarrassing — they can be Brady material, expose agencies to civil liability, and cause wrongful charges.
• Read the full report: AI-Generated Police Reports — High Tech, Low Accuracy, Big Risks (June 2025)
2c. So what now? Using AI safely in public safety
💡 The risks are real — and that's exactly why structured training matters
The question is not whether to use AI — departments are already using it, and the efficiency gains are real. The question is how to use it without creating new harms. Ignoring AI leaves officers under-equipped. Using it carelessly transfers legal and ethical risk to the department and the individuals whose cases it touches.
Three principles for safe use:
1. Human-in-the-loop is non-negotiable for evidentiary output. AI drafts; a human reviews, corrects, and signs. No AI output enters a legal record without officer attestation. The Code Four frog incident is a classroom example of why.
2. Use AI where the cost of error is recoverable. Longeye processing 60 hours of recordings is a green-light use — a human still reviews leads. An AI auto-generating probable cause statements is red-light — the cost of error is a wrongful arrest.
3. Build the audit habit before the tool arrives. Train responders to ask: What did the AI add that was not in my source notes? That question catches hallucinations. Without the habit, officers will unknowingly sign off on AI inventions.
The university role: We are not here to sell tools or to ban them. We are here to give departments the evaluation framework to know the difference between a Longeye (evidence-scale speed gain, recoverable error) and an unvetted report-writer (legal minefield). That judgment cannot be outsourced to a vendor.
Workshop takeaway prompt: Name one task in your shift where AI saves time without creating legal risk — and one where it must never touch the output.

About this scenario bank

Scenario 1 The Hallucinated DUI Report Police
Context
Your department uses Code Four to transcribe body-worn camera audio and draft incident reports. After a DUI stop, the AI produces the draft below. Your job before signing: find every claim the AI added that was not in the source notes.
Source notes (what the officer recorded)
Subject was weaving. Stopped at 22:14. Officer Miller approached. Subject smells of alcohol. Subject stated "I only had two beers at the Mariners game." Officer Miller noted a half-empty can of Rainier on the passenger floor. Subject failed the walk-and-turn.
AI-generated draft (Code Four output)
On October 14, 2025, at approximately 10:14 PM, Officer Miller initiated a traffic stop on a blue sedan for reckless driving. Upon contact, the driver admitted to heavy drinking at a sporting event. Officer Miller observed multiple open containers in the vehicle. The suspect was unable to stand during field sobriety tests.
Example participant prompt to the AI
Participant
Compare the AI draft to the source notes line by line. Flag every claim in the draft that is not explicitly supported by the source. List each flag with the draft phrase and what the source actually says.
AI response
Here are the unsupported claims I found:

1. "reckless driving" — source says "weaving," which is a different legal standard.
2. "admitted to heavy drinking" — source says subject stated "I only had two beers," which is a denial, not an admission.
3. "multiple open containers" — source mentions one half-empty can of Rainier.
4. "unable to stand" — source says "failed the walk-and-turn," which is a specific standardized test result, not a general inability to stand.

I also note the vehicle color (blue sedan) and date (October 14) are not present in the source notes. These may be accurate but cannot be verified from the notes provided.
Work through it first — then check.
Facilitator answer key
🔴
"reckless driving" — The source says "weaving." Reckless driving is a distinct charge with a higher legal threshold. An AI substituting the wrong charge creates Brady risk and can undermine the stop's probable cause.
🔴
"admitted to heavy drinking" — The subject explicitly denied: "I only had two beers." Flipping a denial into an admission is a severe hallucination that could constitute a false statement in a legal record.
🔴
"multiple open containers" — One half-empty can was noted. "Multiple" is an invention. Under evidence rules, this discrepancy allows defense to challenge the entire report's accuracy.
🔴
"unable to stand" — The officer noted a failed walk-and-turn, a standardized 8-clue test. "Unable to stand" implies gross impairment not documented. These are not the same claim.
💡
Discussion prompt: Defense counsel can use any one of these four errors to impeach Officer Miller on the stand — even if Miller never read the AI draft carefully. This is why AI reports require full human redline before signing, not just a quick skim.
Scenario 2 The 60-Hour Call Stack Investigations
Context
A detective is using Longeye to review phone evidence in a fraud investigation. The AI processes three days of recorded calls and produces a summary flagging one person — "Marcus T." — as a likely co-conspirator. Before requesting a warrant, the detective must verify whether the AI's flag is supported by the actual calls.
Call excerpts (three of sixty hours)
Call A — Day 1, 14:32: "Yeah, the transfer went through. Marcus handled the routing like we talked." Call B — Day 2, 09:15: "I told Marcus to keep his name off the account. He said he would." Call C — Day 3, 21:08: "You talked to Marcus yet? He confirmed the third drop. We are good to go."
AI summary (Longeye output)
Summary: Marcus T. is referenced in three calls across the monitoring period. He is described as routing transfers (Call A), managing account registration (Call B), and confirming a third drop location (Call C). Based on call frequency and role references, Marcus T. appears to be a central coordinator in the scheme.
Example participant prompts to verify
Participant
For each call in the summary, quote the exact phrase from the transcript that supports the AI claim. Do not paraphrase.
AI response
Call A: "Marcus handled the routing like we talked" — supports the routing claim.
Call B: "I told Marcus to keep his name off the account" — supports account management reference.
Call C: I cannot locate a transcript for a third call on Day 3 at 21:08 in the provided excerpts. The "third drop" claim in the summary does not appear to be grounded in the supplied call data.
Participant
Is Marcus T. ever directly identified by last name in any of the calls?
AI response
No. The calls reference "Marcus" only. The last name "T." in my summary was inferred from other case metadata, not from the call transcripts themselves. This should be verified against confirmed identification before inclusion in a warrant application.
What risks did the AI introduce?
Facilitator answer key
✅
Calls A and B are accurately summarized. The AI correctly extracted the relevant references and the summary reflects what was said.
🔴
Call C does not exist in the provided data. The AI fabricated or hallucinated a supporting call. A warrant application citing "three corroborating calls" when one is invented would be a material misrepresentation — a Fourth Amendment issue.
🔴
The last name "T." was inferred, not spoken. AI tools that cross-reference metadata can silently merge uncertain identifications. If "Marcus" in the calls is a different person than the suspect named Marcus T. in the case file, the entire summary points at the wrong individual.
💡
Key lesson: Longeye-style tools are powerful for surfacing leads across large call volumes — but every AI-flagged claim must be traced back to a timestamped, quotable transcript excerpt before it enters a legal document. The AI is a search accelerant, not a fact certifier.
Scenario 3 The Language-Barrier 911 Call EMS / Dispatch
Context
A 911 call comes in from a Spanish-speaking caller. Using Prepared, the dispatch AI provides a real-time English translation. The dispatcher must decide which unit to send based on the translation. What did the AI get right — and what did it miss?
Original Spanish transcript
Caller: "Por favor ayuda, mi abuelo se cayó en el baño. Tiene 78 años. Se está poniendo azul y no responde cuando le hablo. Está respirando pero muy despacio."
AI translation (Prepared output)
Please help, my grandfather fell in the bathroom. He is 78 years old. He is not responding when I talk to him. He is breathing but very slowly.
Example participant prompts
Participant
Compare the original Spanish to the translation word by word. List any phrase in the original that does not appear in the translation.
AI response
The phrase "se está poniendo azul" — meaning "he is turning blue" — is present in the original Spanish but was omitted from the translation. This phrase describes cyanosis, a sign of oxygen deprivation, and is clinically significant.
Participant
What is the clinical significance of "turning blue" in this context?
AI response
Cyanosis — bluish discoloration of skin or lips — indicates inadequate oxygenation. Combined with unresponsiveness and very slow breathing in a 78-year-old fall patient, this profile suggests possible cardiac arrest or severe respiratory compromise. The appropriate response escalates to ALS (Advanced Life Support) priority rather than a standard fall response.
One missed phrase changes everything.
Facilitator answer key
🔴
"Se está poniendo azul" was dropped. The AI translated the call accurately except for this phrase. "Turning blue" is a cyanosis indicator — one of the clearest pre-arrest signs. Without it, the dispatch profile looks like a fall with AMS (altered mental status). With it, the profile is a potential cardiac arrest requiring immediate ALS and pre-notification to the receiving hospital.
🔴
Why it likely happened: Consumer-grade translation models can omit descriptive color/sensation phrases that seem incidental in general conversation but are critical in medical triage vocabulary. Medical AI translation requires domain-specific validation.
✅
What Prepared does well here: The call was triaged in real time without waiting for a human interpreter, and the core facts (fall, elderly, unresponsive, slow breathing) were captured. That alone accelerates dispatch. But dispatchers using AI translation must be trained to ask one follow-up: "Did the caller describe any color change or appearance change to the patient?"
💡
Takeaway for protocol: AI translation is green-light for initial triage but requires a dispatcher trained to probe for omissions — especially color, pain level, and timeline phrases — before finalizing the dispatch code.
Scenario 4 The Bias Audit Leadership
Context
A vendor is pitching a "patrol optimization" AI tool to your department. The tool predicts where incidents are most likely to occur and recommends where to concentrate patrol resources. They provide a summary of the training data. Your task: decide whether to approve for pilot or reject for bias risk.
Vendor training data summary
Training dataset: 4 years of departmental incident reports (2019–2023), 187,000 records. Geographic distribution of "high-risk" classifications: - District 2 (Central): 38% of flagged incidents - District 7 (South): 21% of flagged incidents - District 4 (Eastside): 14% of flagged incidents - All other 17 districts: 27% combined Vendor claim: "Our model has 91% accuracy in predicting where the next incident will occur within a 4-hour window."
Example participant questions at the vendor meeting
Participant
Your training data is 73% concentrated in three districts. Were those districts historically over-patrolled compared to the rest of the city? If more officers are in a district, more incidents get logged there — even if underlying crime rates are similar across districts.
Vendor AI demo
Our model is trained on reported incidents. Patrol distribution in the historical period is outside the scope of the dataset we were provided.
Participant
Have you validated your predictions against victimization survey data — what residents report experiencing — rather than only what officers logged?
Vendor AI demo
We have not incorporated victimization survey data in this version. That is a roadmap item.
Approve for pilot, or reject for bias risk?
Facilitator answer key
🔴
Recommended decision: Reject for bias risk. The training data reflects arrest and incident report density, which is a function of historical patrol density — not underlying community safety conditions. Three districts with known histories of concentrated policing account for 73% of the model inputs.
🔴
The feedback loop risk: Deploying the model will send more officers to Districts 2, 7, and 4. More officers means more logged incidents. More logged incidents reinforces the model. The tool will predict what the patrol pattern created — not what the community actually experiences.
🔴
"91% accuracy" is not a safety claim. If the model accurately predicts where officers will be deployed (because it was trained on that pattern), accuracy is high but the measure is circular. Ask: 91% accuracy compared to what baseline?
✅
Path to approval: Require the vendor to retrain or validate using victimization survey data, 911 call volume (not just arrests), and geographic equity metrics. Add a mandatory bias audit at 6-month intervals. Require explanation of any recommended patrol concentration that deviates from current deployment by more than 15%.
💡
Seattle context: This scenario mirrors the concerns that led Mayor Wilson to pause surveillance expansions in March 2026. The Privacy Review Process required by Seattle IT exists precisely for this type of vendor evaluation.
Scenario 5 The Safe Prompt Challenge All Tracks
Context
An officer hands you their raw on-scene notes from a disturbance call. Your challenge: write a prompt that produces a legally safe draft narrative. Compare the output from an unsafe prompt versus a safe one — and identify what makes the difference.
Raw officer notes
Arrived 14:02. Victim has laceration on left forearm. Suspect fled north on 3rd Ave. Blue jacket. Witness Smith says she saw a knife. No knife recovered. Victim declined transport. Medic 7 on scene, cleared at 14:28.
Unsafe prompt vs. safe prompt — side by side
Unsafe prompt
Write a formal police incident narrative from these notes.
AI output (unsafe)
At approximately 2:02 PM, officers responded to a reported stabbing at the intersection. Upon arrival, they located a victim with a laceration, likely caused by a knife wielded by the fleeing suspect. The suspect, a male in a blue jacket, fled northbound on 3rd Avenue following the attack. Witness Smith confirmed she observed the suspect brandishing a knife. The victim was treated on scene and released by Medic 7 at 2:28 PM.
Safe prompt
Using only the facts listed in these notes — and no other information — write a formal incident narrative in chronological order. Do not infer cause, intent, or weapon unless explicitly stated. For any detail you cannot verify from the notes, insert [UNVERIFIED]. Add "DRAFT — REQUIRES OFFICER VERIFICATION" at the top.
AI output (safe)
DRAFT — REQUIRES OFFICER VERIFICATION

At 14:02, officers arrived on scene. A victim was observed with a laceration on the left forearm. Cause of the laceration was not documented in the notes [UNVERIFIED]. A suspect described as wearing a blue jacket had fled northbound on 3rd Avenue prior to officer arrival. Witness Smith stated she saw a knife; no knife was recovered on scene. The victim declined transport. Medic 7 responded and cleared the scene at 14:28.
Count the problems in the unsafe output.
Facilitator answer key
🔴
"likely caused by a knife" — The notes say only that a witness saw a knife. No knife was recovered. The AI inferred the weapon and causation. This is invented probable cause.
🔴
"confirmed she observed the suspect brandishing a knife" — Smith said she "saw a knife." "Brandishing" implies intentional threatening display — a legal conclusion not in the notes.
🔴
"following the attack" — The notes describe a suspect who fled and a victim with a laceration. The word "attack" is an inference of intent and sequence not documented anywhere.
✅
What the safe prompt adds: (1) "only facts listed in these notes" constrains the context window; (2) "do not infer cause, intent, or weapon" directly blocks the three hallucination patterns; (3) "[UNVERIFIED]" forces explicit flagging; (4) the DRAFT header ensures no one signs it without reading it.
💡
Workshop exercise: Have participants write their own safe prompt before seeing this one — then compare. Key question: which constraint clause catches the most hallucinations? (Usually "do not infer cause or intent.")
Questions to resolve before finalizing the workshop
About the audience
Is this a mixed SPD/SFD/EMS room, or single-department?
Mixed rooms benefit from shared scenarios (hallucination redline, bias audit) but need role breakouts for CJIS vs. HIPAA distinctions. Single-department sessions can go deeper on tools and legal constraints specific to that role.
Are patrol officers, detectives, and command attending together?
Front-line responders need hands-on redline practice. Command needs vendor evaluation framing. Mixing both without a structured agenda risks losing each group — consider parallel tracks or a sequenced agenda.
Have participants already encountered AI tools in their workflow?
If Code Four, Axon, or a similar tool is already live, lead with their real experience. If no tools are deployed yet, frame the workshop as preparation — "here is what is coming and how to evaluate it safely."
Are attendees volunteers or required to attend?
Mandatory training for first responders needs stronger interactive design — scenario work and small-group debate hold attention better than lecture. Volunteer attendees are self-selected motivated learners; go deeper faster.
About the content
Which AI tools are already approved or under evaluation by the department?
Grounding scenarios in tools the department is actually considering makes the workshop immediately actionable. A vendor evaluation exercise using a real pending proposal is far more valuable than a hypothetical one.
What is the current KCPAO stance on AI-generated reports and evidence?
The King County Prosecutor's Office position on AI-assisted narratives directly shapes the legal risk framing. If their stance has shifted since this document was drafted, update the Traffic Light framework accordingly.
Should we use Seattle IT policy documents as workshop material?
Using official Seattle AI policy text increases institutional credibility — but may require legal or communications clearance to reproduce in handouts. Confirm before printing.
About the format
How long do we have — and is it one session or a recurring series?
A lightning talk at roll call is 30–45 minutes. A half-day workshop needs 3–4 hours and a classroom. A learning series needs a department cohort committed for 5 weeks. Each requires a different level of organizer coordination with SPD/SFD/EMS scheduling.
In-person, virtual, or hybrid?
Hands-on redline exercises and small-group debate are significantly harder in hybrid. If hybrid delivery is required, use Miro or Mentimeter for scenario voting and a structured debrief protocol to keep remote participants engaged.
About the outcome
What do we want participants to be able to DO after the workshop?
"Know more about AI" is too vague. Concrete targets: catch a hallucination in an AI report before signing, identify a CJIS violation in a proposed tool deployment, write a safe prompt that constrains AI output to source facts only. Pick one or two and design backward from them.
Is there a follow-on action — a field card, a CoP, a department policy review?
A workshop with a clear next step retains learning far better than a standalone event. The Seattle AI Incident Card (in Sample Workshop #1) is a ready-made takeaway. A community of practice connecting front-line responders to Seattle IT creates a lasting feedback loop.
How will we capture real hallucination examples from participants for future workshops?
First responders who encounter AI errors in the field have the most credible real-world examples. Build a lightweight reporting path — a shared form or debrief channel — so their field observations feed back into the next workshop iteration.
