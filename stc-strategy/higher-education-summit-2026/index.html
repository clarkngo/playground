STC is a private institution within the City University of Seattle system, serving a student body that includes a significant international and non-native English-speaking population. This context is important: equity considerations around AI access and AI prompting skills apply directly to STC’s students in ways they may not for large public research universities.

At the summit, STC was represented by Clark (and Morad, in the closing discussion). In Session 1 and 2, STC’s approach was described as one of the more advanced in the room — employer credits, BSAI/MSAI pathways, AI workshops for faculty, and a functioning AI committee. The summit confirmed this positioning while also surfacing areas where STC’s work is incomplete.

02 Summit Validated

These are things STC is already doing that multiple summit speakers independently identified as best practice. No action required — but worth naming explicitly because they justify continued investment.

AI infusion across existing programs — not just a standalone AI course, but embedding AI across the curriculum. Ingrid, Lauren, and Kristin all described this approach as the right direction. STC’s "AI in X / X in AI" framing is exactly this.
Standalone AI degree pathways (BSAI, MSAI) — Rovy noted that micro-credentials and specialized programs are the nimble layer. Patrick described AI degree programs as a legitimate leapfrog. STC already has both.
AI committee with diverse voices — Kristin said explicitly that AI resistors are important to the conversation and produce better outcomes than top-down mandates. STC’s committee structure, including skeptics, is the right model.
Portfolio-based, AI-resistant assessments — Liz, Kirk, and Rovy all converged on this: move away from grades-as-proxy, toward documented process and demonstrated understanding. STC is already building this direction into BSAI.
Open-source LLM deployment (Ollama) — Patrick’s ASU example of an AI agent that simulates a patient for medical students illustrates the principle: build practical AI tools into the learning environment directly. STC’s local Ollama deployment is infrastructure for this.
Faculty AI workshops — Kristin described a summer institute model for faculty. STC’s existing AI workshops for educators are aligned. The summit confirmed these need to be ongoing, not one-time events.
Referencing peer institutions for credibility — STC references ASU, UW, and Stanford to make the case internally. The summit included speakers from ASU, UW Continuum, and Bellevue College — the same peer set. This benchmarking approach is appropriate and effective.
Project-based, hands-on learning (HOS, CS628) — Patrick described ASU’s AI simulation patient as a high-value project-based learning experience. STC’s HOS lab structure (CS445, CS628) already builds practical skills in a structured, hands-on format. The CS628 module that goes from Slack conversation to deployed RAG application is this model at its best.
03 Gaps Surfaced

These are areas the summit highlighted as critical that STC has not yet fully addressed. These are not failures — they are the next layer of work.

No repeatable employer co-design process — Lauren said programs co-designed with employers in the first quarter succeed at higher rates. James confirmed there is no consistent, repeatable template for this anywhere. STC has employer relationships (Boeing, tech companies providing credits) but no formal co-design structure built into the program development cycle.
Summit reference: Panel 1, Session 1 (James, Lauren)
No shared institutional definition of an "AI-ready STC graduate" — Shannon asked what AI literacy even means; the summit could not agree. STC has not yet produced a competency framework that answers this for its own programs. Without one, "AI infusion" risks meaning different things in different courses with no through-line.
Summit reference: Session 2 (Clark, Bill), Session 3 (Shannon, Clark), Closing (Lisa Power)
No explicit equity strategy for international and ESL students — CityU serves a large international student population. Libby noted that workers whose first language is not English are the least equipped to prompt AI effectively. Rosella described students from countries where education was restricted. STC has no documented approach to scaffolding AI tool use for these students specifically.
Summit reference: Panel 1 (Libby), Session 1 (Rosella)
Course approval process is the main bottleneck — and it is not solved — Clark named this explicitly in Session 2. The summit did not offer a direct solution, but Kristin’s point is relevant: institutions that stall on shared governance are falling behind. The bottleneck needs a workaround or a process fix, not just patience.
Summit reference: Session 2 (Clark), Panel 2 (Kristin)
No per-course AI use policy (red/yellow/green) — Gabrielle noted that colleges are still figuring out AI policies for faculty and students. STC has an AI committee and broad direction, but individual courses may not have explicit, communicated AI use expectations. This creates inconsistency for students across the program.
Summit reference: Panel 2 (Kristin), Session 2 (Gabrielle)
Faculty change management is under-resourced relative to the pace of change — Rovy and Kristin both said this is more a change management challenge than a tool challenge. Faculty are redesigning curricula repeatedly. STC has workshops, but the summit suggests the need is for sustained support structures — time, space, and voice — not just training sessions.
Summit reference: Panel 2 (Kristin, Rovy, Liz)
No K–12 or feeder pipeline engagement — Kirk raised in the closing: are these conversations happening at the K–12 level? If students arrive at STC without foundational AI fluency, STC absorbs that deficit. CityU’s pipeline from high school is not a strength of the current transformation strategy.
Summit reference: Closing (Kirk), Panel 2 (Kristin)
04 Mapped Opportunities

Specific summit insights mapped to STC programs, courses, and initiatives. Each includes a concrete application and a suggested action.

🎈
Red/Yellow/Green Assignment Tagging → CS445 & CS628
From: Kristin Copeland, Clover Park Technical College (Panel 2)
Kristin’s three-tier AI tagging system for assignments is immediately adoptable. Green = use AI freely, Yellow = use AI in a specific defined way, Red = do not use AI (it will impede the learning). It gives students clarity, gives faculty control, and keeps the learning goal in view rather than treating AI as categorically good or bad. CS445 and CS628 both have enough assignment variety that all three tiers would apply naturally.
Pilot the tagging system in CS445 HOS assignments and CS628 module deliverables. Document which assignments land in each tier and share with the AI committee as a model for other courses.
🧠
Bloom’s Taxonomy of AI → BSAI Competency Framework
From: Lisa Power, Saint Martin’s University (Closing Discussion)
Lisa Power proposed developing a hierarchical AI competency framework modeled on Bloom’s Taxonomy — from basic AI awareness up through applied AI creation. STC is in the best position of any institution in this region to build this, given the BSAI program already under development. A published framework would serve as both an internal curriculum alignment tool and an external signal to employers about what an STC AI graduate can do.
Draft a "Bloom’s Taxonomy of AI" competency map as part of the BSAI program documentation. Map existing BSAI courses to levels of the framework. Share with the AI committee and, eventually, employer partners for validation.
📊
Multi-AI Comparison → AI Literacy Lab (CS445 or BSAI)
From: Clark & Shannon, Session 3 discussion
Running the same prompt through multiple AI models (Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, a local Ollama model) and comparing outputs is a concrete, tool-agnostic AI literacy exercise. It teaches students to notice bias, evaluate outputs critically, and understand that AI is not a neutral oracle. STC’s Ollama deployment makes a local-vs-cloud comparison uniquely possible here. This maps directly to the dual-AI cultural simulation idea raised in networking conversations.
Build a standalone HOS activity: "Multi-AI Comparison Lab." Students submit the same prompt to three or more models, document differences in tone, accuracy, and framing, and write a brief analysis. Add to the playground as a new HOS module.
👥
Employer Co-Design in Q1 → BSAI Curriculum Review
From: Lauren Van Koughnett & James Bowen, Panel 1 & Session 1
Lauren’s finding: programs co-designed with employers in the first quarter of development are consistently more successful. James proposed a concrete method: deconstruct job descriptions into component skills, then reconstruct those skills into curriculum or assessments. STC already has employer relationships (Boeing, tech companies offering free credits). The missing piece is formalizing these relationships into a structured curriculum review process, not just informal conversations.
Identify two to three employer partners already engaged with STC. Invite them into a structured Q1 curriculum review session for the BSAI program. Use James’s job-description deconstruction method as the working format. Document the process so it is repeatable for future cohorts.
🎯
Passion-Led Assessment → CS628 Capstone Redesign
From: Dr. Liz Hollerman, Bellevue College (Panel 2)
Liz described assigning students to research a nonprofit connected to their own background and community. When students have genuine personal stakes in the subject, the learning becomes unmistakable and self-sustaining. CS628’s capstone is already project-based, but the project topic is typically instructor-assigned or predetermined. Giving students agency to connect their capstone to a real organization in their own community — a company, nonprofit, or industry from their background — could significantly raise engagement and the quality of demonstrated learning.
Introduce a "connect to your context" option in the CS628 capstone: students propose a real-world organization (company, nonprofit, or community project) as the basis for their full-stack application. Pair with a brief proposal and reflection component to make the stakes visible.
⚠
Pre-Mortem Exercise → BSAI Program Review
From: Robert (Closing Discussion)
Robert proposed a pre-mortem: work backward from a failure scenario to identify how AI might go wrong across employers, higher education, and K–12 before committing to a direction. Applied to BSAI: imagine it is 2028 and the BSAI program has failed to produce graduates that employers want. What went wrong? What assumptions did we make in 2026 that turned out to be false? This exercise surfaces risks that forward-looking planning tends to miss, and is well suited to an AI committee session.
Run a 90-minute pre-mortem session with the AI committee, focused on the BSAI program. Document the failure scenarios identified and use them to stress-test the current curriculum and employer alignment strategy.
🌐
Reciprocal Transparency → STC AI Use Norm
From: Mike Meotti, Keynote
Meotti’s reciprocal transparency principle — faculty disclose AI use, students disclose AI use — is a cultural norm, not a policy. STC’s AI committee is the right body to adopt and model this norm first, then cascade it to faculty and students. Paired with the red/yellow/green tagging system, this creates a coherent AI culture: visible, honest, and non-adversarial.
Propose a reciprocal transparency norm to the AI committee. Draft a one-page statement for faculty to share at the start of each course: "Here is how I use AI in designing this course. Here is what I expect from you." Encourage students to respond in kind.
🗣
AI as Safe Space for Low-Confidence Learners → International Student Support
From: Mike Nielsen & Shannon, Session 3
Mike observed that AI can serve as a safe space for students who lack the confidence to ask a human instructor. This is especially relevant for STC’s international students, who may face cultural or language barriers to asking questions in class. Rather than treating AI as a threat to learning, STC can position it explicitly as a first-contact support tool that lowers the barrier to engagement — with the expectation that students eventually bring those questions back to instructors or peers.
In orientation materials and course syllabi, explicitly frame AI tools as a low-stakes first step for asking questions — not a replacement for instructor engagement, but a bridge to it. Pair with guidance on effective prompting for ESL contexts.
05 Open Questions for STC

These are the summit’s unanswered questions, reframed for STC specifically. They belong in AI committee discussions or program review sessions — not because there are easy answers, but because STC needs to develop its own answers rather than importing someone else’s.

What does an AI-ready STC graduate actually look like — and what is our evidence that our programs are producing one?
The summit could not agree on a definition. STC is better positioned than most to define this for its own graduates. The BSAI program needs this definition to exist before it can claim to deliver it.
What is STC’s traffic-light policy for AI use across courses — and who communicates it to students consistently?
Individual faculty are setting their own policies without coordination. Students moving from CS445 to CS628 to BSAI capstone may encounter contradictory expectations. A shared framework, even a flexible one, is better than silence.
How does STC support students whose first language is not English in developing AI prompting skills — not just language skills?
Effective AI use requires being able to articulate what you want. Students who struggle to express themselves in English will get worse AI outputs, reinforcing disadvantage. This is a design problem, not just a language support problem.
What is the irreducible human contribution in an STC classroom — what would we insist on keeping human even if AI could do it?
This question was never asked directly at the summit. STC should ask it. The answer determines which parts of the curriculum are AI-augmentable and which are not, and shapes what faculty development should actually prioritize.
If the BSAI program fails by 2028, what went wrong? What assumptions are we making today that might not hold?
The pre-mortem question. This is not pessimism — it is risk management. Employer needs may shift faster than the program can respond. The course approval bottleneck may stall critical updates. AI tool fluency without critical reasoning may produce graduates employers don’t want.
How do we formalize employer input into program design — not as a courtesy, but as a structural requirement in the first quarter of any new program?
STC has employer relationships. The missing piece is making co-design a process, not an event. Lauren’s evidence is clear: programs designed with employers in Q1 succeed. Programs designed without employer input are playing catch-up from day one.
Are we engaging students upstream — in high school or community college — or are we inheriting their AI literacy deficit by default?
Kirk raised this in the closing. CityU is a private institution without obvious K–12 partnership structures. But if students arrive without basic AI fluency, STC absorbs that problem. Even lightweight engagement — a webinar, a short course, a pipeline program — could shift this.
